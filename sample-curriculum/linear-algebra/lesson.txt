LESSON: Linear Algebra
LANGUAGE: en

SLIDE 1 | Welcome to Linear Algebra
Linear algebra is the study of vectors, matrices, and linear transformations. It is one of the
most widely applied areas of mathematics, powering machine learning, computer graphics,
engineering, economics, and data science. Its key questions revolve around solving systems of
equations and understanding structure in higher dimensions.
NARRATION: Linear algebra provides the mathematical framework behind machine learning, computer graphics, and data science.

SLIDE 2 | Systems of Linear Equations
A system of linear equations is a collection of equations that must all be satisfied
simultaneously. A system can have exactly one solution, infinitely many solutions, or no
solution at all. Geometric intuition: each equation represents a line (in 2D) or a plane
(in 3D), and solutions are their intersections.
NARRATION: Every system of equations is either consistent with one or many solutions, or inconsistent with no solution.

SLIDE 3 | Row Reduction and Echelon Form
Row reduction uses three elementary row operations — swapping rows, multiplying a row by a
nonzero scalar, and adding a multiple of one row to another — to transform a matrix into
row echelon form (REF) or reduced row echelon form (RREF). The algorithm is called
Gaussian elimination (or Gauss-Jordan for RREF).
NARRATION: Row reduction systematically eliminates variables to simplify a system into a form that is easy to solve.

SLIDE 4 | Matrix Notation and Basic Operations
A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers. An m×n matrix has m rows and n columns. You can
add two matrices of the same size by adding corresponding entries, and multiply a matrix by a
scalar by scaling every entry. Subtraction and scalar operations follow the same element-wise rule.
NARRATION: Matrices organize numbers in rows and columns; addition and scalar multiplication work entry by entry.

SLIDE 5 | Matrix Multiplication
The product AB is defined when the number of columns of A equals the number of rows of B.
Entry (i, j) of AB is the dot product of the ith row of A with the jth column of B. Matrix
multiplication is associative and distributes over addition, but it is not commutative in general.
NARRATION: Matrix multiplication takes dot products of rows with columns; always check that the inner dimensions match.

SLIDE 6 | The Inverse of a Matrix
A square matrix A is invertible if there exists a matrix A⁻¹ such that AA⁻¹ = A⁻¹A = I,
where I is the identity matrix. To find A⁻¹, augment A with I and row-reduce until the left
side becomes I; the right side then holds A⁻¹. A matrix is invertible if and only if its
determinant is nonzero.
NARRATION: The inverse of a matrix undoes its effect; row-reduce the augmented matrix to compute it.

SLIDE 7 | Determinants of 2×2 Matrices
For a 2×2 matrix [[a, b], [c, d]], the determinant is det(A) = ad − bc. The determinant gives
the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by the column vectors. A nonzero determinant
means the matrix is invertible; a zero determinant means the columns are linearly dependent.
NARRATION: The 2-by-2 determinant is ad minus bc and measures the area-scaling factor of the linear transformation.

SLIDE 8 | Determinants of 3×3 Matrices
A 3×3 determinant is computed by cofactor expansion along any row or column. Expanding along
the first row gives det(A) = a₁₁C₁₁ + a₁₂C₁₂ + a₁₃C₁₃, where each cofactor C_{ij} is
(−1)^{i+j} times the determinant of the 2×2 minor obtained by deleting row i and column j.
NARRATION: Expand a 3-by-3 determinant along a row or column by multiplying each entry by its cofactor and summing.

SLIDE 9 | Cramer's Rule
For a square invertible system Ax = b, Cramer's Rule says the solution is x_i = det(A_i)/det(A),
where A_i is the matrix A with its ith column replaced by b. Although computationally expensive
for large systems, Cramer's Rule is elegant and useful for 2×2 and 3×3 systems.
NARRATION: Cramer's Rule expresses each variable as a ratio of determinants, replacing the corresponding column with the right-hand side.

SLIDE 10 | Vectors in Rⁿ
A vector in Rⁿ is an ordered list of n real numbers. Vectors can be added component-wise and
scaled by any real number. The zero vector is the additive identity. Geometrically, a vector
represents a direction and magnitude, or equivalently a point in n-dimensional space.
NARRATION: Vectors are lists of numbers you can add and scale; they represent points and directions in any number of dimensions.

SLIDE 11 | Vector Spaces and Subspaces
A vector space is a set of vectors closed under addition and scalar multiplication, satisfying
ten axioms. A subspace is a subset that is itself a vector space (contains the zero vector, and
is closed under addition and scalar multiplication). Key examples are the null space and column
space of a matrix.
NARRATION: A subspace must contain zero and be closed under addition and scaling — it is a flat structure passing through the origin.

SLIDE 12 | Linear Independence
A set of vectors {v₁, …, v_k} is linearly independent if the only solution to c₁v₁ + … + c_k v_k = 0
is c₁ = … = c_k = 0. Otherwise the set is linearly dependent, meaning at least one vector can
be written as a combination of the others. Independence tests reduce to row reduction.
NARRATION: Vectors are linearly independent when no vector in the set can be built from the others using scaling and adding.

SLIDE 13 | Span and Basis
The span of a set of vectors is all possible linear combinations of those vectors. A basis for
a subspace is a linearly independent set that spans the subspace. Every basis for a given
subspace has the same number of vectors, which defines the dimension of that subspace.
NARRATION: A basis is the minimal spanning set — just enough independent vectors to reach every point in the subspace.

SLIDE 14 | Dimension, Rank, and Nullity
The rank of a matrix A is the dimension of its column space (equal to the number of pivot
columns after row reduction). The nullity is the dimension of the null space (solutions to
Ax = 0). The Rank-Nullity Theorem states rank + nullity = n, where n is the number of columns.
NARRATION: Rank plus nullity always equals the number of columns — this is the Rank-Nullity Theorem.

SLIDE 15 | Linear Transformations
A function T: Rⁿ → Rᵐ is a linear transformation if T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v) and T(cu) = cT(u)
for all vectors u, v and scalar c. Every linear transformation can be represented by matrix
multiplication: T(x) = Ax. Composition of transformations corresponds to matrix multiplication.
NARRATION: Linear transformations preserve addition and scaling, and every one of them can be encoded as a matrix.

SLIDE 16 | Null Space and Column Space
The null space (kernel) of A is {x : Ax = 0}; it describes the inputs that map to zero. The
column space (image) of A is the span of A's columns; it describes all possible outputs. Row
reduction on A reveals both: pivot columns span the column space; free variables parameterize the null space.
NARRATION: The null space is all inputs that get squashed to zero; the column space is all vectors the transformation can produce.

SLIDE 17 | Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors
A nonzero vector v is an eigenvector of A with eigenvalue λ if Av = λv. Eigenvalues are found
by solving det(A − λI) = 0, the characteristic equation. Each eigenvalue then yields eigenvectors
by solving (A − λI)v = 0. Eigenvectors point in directions that the transformation merely scales.
NARRATION: Eigenvectors are special directions that a matrix only stretches or flips, never rotates — and eigenvalues give the scale factor.

SLIDE 18 | Diagonalization
A matrix A is diagonalizable if there exists an invertible matrix P (whose columns are
eigenvectors) such that P⁻¹AP = D, where D is a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. Diagonalization
simplifies matrix powers: Aⁿ = PDⁿP⁻¹, making it easy to compute exponentials and
long-run behaviors.
NARRATION: Diagonalization rewrites a matrix in its eigenvector basis where it acts by simple scalings along each axis.

SLIDE 19 | The Dot Product and Orthogonality
The dot product of u and v is u · v = u₁v₁ + … + u_n v_n = |u||v|cos θ. Two vectors are
orthogonal if their dot product is zero. The norm (length) of v is |v| = √(v · v). Orthogonality
is the generalization of perpendicularity to any number of dimensions.
NARRATION: Two vectors are orthogonal when their dot product is zero — a generalization of right angles to n dimensions.

SLIDE 20 | Orthogonal Projections
The orthogonal projection of vector b onto vector a is proj_a b = (b·a / a·a) a. This gives
the component of b pointing in the direction of a. Projections onto subspaces are computed by
multiplying with the projection matrix P = A(AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ, whose columns span the subspace.
NARRATION: An orthogonal projection finds the closest point in a subspace to a given vector, measured by straight-line distance.

SLIDE 21 | The Gram-Schmidt Process
Given a basis {v₁, …, v_k} for a subspace, Gram-Schmidt constructs an orthonormal basis
{e₁, …, e_k}. At each step, subtract from the next vector its projections onto all previous
orthonormal vectors. The result is a set of mutually perpendicular unit vectors spanning the
same subspace.
NARRATION: Gram-Schmidt systematically removes components to build an orthogonal — and then normalized — basis step by step.

SLIDE 22 | Least Squares Problems
When the system Ax = b has no solution (overdetermined), the least-squares solution minimizes
|Ax − b|². It satisfies the normal equations AᵀAx̂ = Aᵀb, so x̂ = (AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀb. Least
squares is the mathematical foundation of linear regression in statistics and machine learning.
NARRATION: The least-squares solution finds the best approximate answer when no exact solution exists — minimizing total squared error.

SLIDE 23 | Symmetric Matrices and the Spectral Theorem
A matrix is symmetric if A = Aᵀ. The Spectral Theorem guarantees that every real symmetric
matrix has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors and all real eigenvalues. This means every
symmetric matrix can be diagonalized as A = QDQᵀ, where Q is orthogonal and D is diagonal.
NARRATION: Symmetric matrices are perfectly diagonalizable by an orthogonal matrix — their eigenvectors are always perpendicular.

SLIDE 24 | Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)
Every m×n matrix A can be factored as A = UΣVᵀ, where U and V are orthogonal matrices and Σ
is a diagonal matrix of non-negative singular values. SVD generalizes eigendecomposition to
non-square matrices and reveals the intrinsic geometry of any linear transformation.
NARRATION: SVD decomposes any matrix into a rotation, a scaling along principal axes, and another rotation — exposing its true structure.

SLIDE 25 | Applications in Data Science and Machine Learning
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) uses SVD to reduce the dimensionality of data by keeping
only the largest singular values. Linear regression uses least squares. Neural networks compose
linear transformations with nonlinear activations. Understanding linear algebra unlocks
deeper intuition for every machine learning algorithm.
NARRATION: PCA, linear regression, and neural networks all rest on linear algebra; understanding the foundations unlocks the algorithms.

SLIDE 26 | Applications in Computer Graphics
Computer graphics use matrices to represent rotations, scaling, shearing, and perspective
projection in 2D and 3D. A chain of transformations is a single matrix product. Homogeneous
coordinates add an extra dimension to unify translation with other linear operations into one
matrix multiplication.
NARRATION: Every rotation, zoom, and perspective shift in a 3D game or animation is computed with matrix multiplication.

FACT: The Rank-Nullity Theorem states that rank plus nullity equals the number of columns.

FACT: Eigenvectors are directions unchanged by a transformation except for scaling by the eigenvalue.
FACT: The Spectral Theorem guarantees an orthonormal eigenvector basis for every real symmetric matrix.
FACT: SVD factors any matrix into U times Sigma times V-transpose.
FACT: Least squares solves the normal equations A-transpose A x-hat equals A-transpose b.
