LESSON: CompTIA A+ IT Certification Prep
LANGUAGE: en

SLIDE 1 | What is CompTIA A+?
CompTIA A+ is an entry-level IT certification that validates foundational skills in
hardware, software, networking, and troubleshooting. It is vendor-neutral and
recognized by employers worldwide. The exam consists of two parts: Core 1 (220-1101)
and Core 2 (220-1102).
NARRATION: CompTIA A+ proves you have the core IT skills employers look for, covering
both hardware and software across two separate exams.

SLIDE 2 | The Central Processing Unit (CPU)
The CPU is the brain of the computer. It executes instructions by fetching, decoding,
and executing them in a cycle. Key specs include clock speed (GHz), number of cores,
cache size (L1, L2, L3), and socket type. Common manufacturers are Intel and AMD.
NARRATION: The CPU performs all calculations; understand clock speed, core count, and
socket compatibility when selecting or replacing one.

SLIDE 3 | RAM - Random Access Memory
RAM is volatile short-term memory used by the CPU to store active data. Common types
include DDR4 and DDR5. Key specs are speed (MHz), capacity (GB), and form factor
(DIMM for desktops, SO-DIMM for laptops). More RAM allows more programs to run
simultaneously.
NARRATION: RAM is temporary working memory; when the power goes off, its contents
are lost.

SLIDE 4 | Storage Devices
Storage devices hold data persistently. HDDs (hard disk drives) use spinning magnetic
platters and are slower but cheaper per gigabyte. SSDs (solid-state drives) use flash
memory and are much faster. NVMe SSDs connect via PCIe for maximum speed. Optical
drives read CDs, DVDs, and Blu-ray discs.
NARRATION: SSDs have replaced HDDs in most modern systems because of their speed and
durability.

SLIDE 5 | Motherboards
The motherboard is the main circuit board that connects all components. Key features
include the CPU socket type, chipset, RAM slots (DIMMs), expansion slots (PCIe), SATA
ports for storage, USB headers, and the BIOS/UEFI firmware chip. Form factors include
ATX, Micro-ATX, and Mini-ITX.
NARRATION: The motherboard is the backbone of the system; every component must be
compatible with its socket and slot types.

SLIDE 6 | Power Supply Units (PSU)
The PSU converts AC power from the wall to DC power for components. Rated in watts,
it must supply enough power for all components. The 80 Plus rating indicates
efficiency. Connectors include 24-pin ATX for the motherboard, 8-pin EPS for the CPU,
and PCIe connectors for GPUs.
NARRATION: Always calculate total system wattage before choosing a PSU to avoid
underpowering your build.

SLIDE 7 | Laptop vs Desktop Components
Laptops use smaller, more power-efficient components. They include integrated batteries,
SO-DIMM RAM, M.2 SSDs, and often have soldered CPUs. Desktops use full-size ATX
components and are easier to upgrade. Laptop screens, keyboards, and touchpads are
integrated and require specialized tools to service.
NARRATION: Servicing laptops requires care because components are compact, often
proprietary, and more fragile than desktop parts.

SLIDE 8 | Display Technologies
LCD (liquid crystal display) panels use a backlight and are the most common type.
IPS panels offer better color and viewing angles. TN panels are faster but have
narrower angles. VA panels provide high contrast. OLED displays produce light
per pixel, enabling true blacks. Resolution terms include HD (1280x720), FHD
(1920x1080), and 4K (3840x2160).
NARRATION: Understanding panel types helps you choose the right display for gaming,
design, or general office use.

SLIDE 9 | Printers and Imaging Devices
Laser printers use toner and a heated fuser to bond images to paper; they are fast
and economical for text. Inkjet printers spray liquid ink and excel at photos.
Thermal printers use heat-sensitive paper for receipts. Impact printers (dot matrix)
strike a ribbon against paper. Scanners and MFPs (multifunction printers) combine
printing, scanning, and copying.
NARRATION: Know the print process for each type: laser uses a drum and fuser, inkjet
uses liquid ink cartridges.

SLIDE 10 | Mobile Devices
Mobile devices include smartphones, tablets, e-readers, smartwatches, and fitness
trackers. They use ARM processors for power efficiency. Common connectivity features
include Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, NFC, cellular (4G LTE, 5G), and GPS. Mobile OS choices
are Android and iOS. Technicians must understand synchronization, mobile device
management (MDM), and app stores.
NARRATION: Mobile devices are everywhere in the enterprise; understanding MDM and
synchronization is essential for A+ technicians.

SLIDE 11 | Networking Fundamentals - TCP/IP
TCP/IP is the protocol suite that powers the internet. IP addresses identify devices
on a network; IPv4 uses 32-bit addresses (e.g., 192.168.1.1) and IPv6 uses 128-bit
addresses. TCP provides reliable, connection-oriented communication. UDP is faster
and connectionless, used for streaming. Common ports include HTTP (80), HTTPS (443),
FTP (21), SSH (22), and DNS (53).
NARRATION: TCP/IP is the language of the internet; memorize key port numbers because
they appear heavily on the exam.

SLIDE 12 | The OSI Model
The OSI model has seven layers: Physical (cables and signals), Data Link (MAC
addresses, switches), Network (IP addresses, routers), Transport (TCP/UDP, ports),
Session (establishing connections), Presentation (encryption, formatting), and
Application (HTTP, FTP, DNS). Use the mnemonic "Please Do Not Throw Sausage Pizza
Away" to remember the order from bottom to top.
NARRATION: The OSI model is a framework for understanding how data travels; each
layer has specific protocols and devices.

SLIDE 13 | Wi-Fi Standards
IEEE 802.11 defines wireless networking. Key standards: 802.11a/g (54 Mbps),
802.11n (Wi-Fi 4, 600 Mbps, 2.4 and 5 GHz), 802.11ac (Wi-Fi 5, multi-gigabit,
5 GHz), and 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6, improved density and efficiency). The 2.4 GHz band
has longer range but is more congested; 5 GHz is faster with shorter range. Security
protocols progress from WEP (weak) to WPA2 and WPA3 (recommended).
NARRATION: Know each Wi-Fi generation's speed and frequency; Wi-Fi 6 is the current
best standard for home and enterprise networks.

SLIDE 14 | Network Hardware
Routers connect different networks and direct traffic using IP addresses. Switches
connect devices within a LAN using MAC addresses. Hubs broadcast to all ports and
are obsolete. Access points extend wireless coverage. Modems convert ISP signals to
Ethernet. Cables include Cat5e (1 Gbps), Cat6 (10 Gbps short run), and fiber optic
for long distances.
NARRATION: Routers work at Layer 3 with IP; switches work at Layer 2 with MAC
addresses.

SLIDE 15 | Windows OS Basics
Windows is the dominant desktop OS. Key tools include Device Manager (hardware
drivers), Task Manager (processes, performance), Control Panel and Settings, Registry
Editor (regedit), and Command Prompt or PowerShell. File system types include NTFS
(modern, supports permissions) and FAT32 (older, limited to 4 GB files). Windows
editions include Home, Pro, and Enterprise with different features.
NARRATION: A+ technicians must navigate Windows confidently, from installing drivers
to editing the registry.

SLIDE 16 | macOS and Linux Basics
macOS is Apple's Unix-based OS used on Mac computers. Key features include Finder,
System Preferences, Time Machine backups, and the Terminal for command-line access.
Linux is open-source and available in distributions like Ubuntu and CentOS. Common
Linux commands include ls (list), cd (change directory), chmod (permissions), and
sudo (superuser). Both are important for the A+ Core 2 exam.
NARRATION: Linux skills are increasingly valuable; know basic terminal commands and
the difference between user and root permissions.

SLIDE 17 | Virtualization
Virtualization allows multiple operating systems to run on one physical host using
a hypervisor. Type 1 hypervisors (bare-metal) run directly on hardware, such as
VMware ESXi. Type 2 hypervisors run within a host OS, such as VirtualBox and
VMware Workstation. VMs (virtual machines) share physical CPU, RAM, and storage.
Benefits include isolation, snapshots, and efficient resource use.
NARRATION: Virtualization lets one machine act as many; snapshots allow you to roll
back a VM to a previous state.

SLIDE 18 | Cloud Computing
Cloud computing delivers computing resources over the internet. Service models include
IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service, e.g., virtual servers), PaaS (Platform as a
Service, e.g., app hosting), and SaaS (Software as a Service, e.g., Google Workspace).
Deployment models include public, private, hybrid, and community clouds. Benefits
include scalability, reduced capital expense, and accessibility.
NARRATION: Know the three cloud service models and deployment types; they appear
on both Core 1 and Core 2.

SLIDE 19 | Cybersecurity Threats
Common threats include phishing (deceptive emails), social engineering (manipulating
users), man-in-the-middle attacks (intercepting traffic), denial-of-service (DoS)
flooding a server, SQL injection targeting databases, and ransomware encrypting files
for payment. Zero-day exploits target unpatched vulnerabilities. Physical threats
include shoulder surfing and tailgating.
NARRATION: Understanding attack types helps you recognize and prevent them; social
engineering exploits people, not technology.

SLIDE 20 | Malware Types and Removal
Malware (malicious software) types include viruses (attach to files), worms (self-
replicate across networks), Trojans (disguised as legitimate software), spyware
(monitors activity), adware (unwanted ads), ransomware (encrypts for ransom), and
rootkits (hide in the OS). Removal steps: disconnect from network, boot to safe mode,
run anti-malware, delete quarantined files, patch and update, and verify clean.
NARRATION: Memorize the malware types and follow the removal process in order; safe
mode prevents malware from loading at startup.

SLIDE 21 | Operational Procedures
Operational procedures include change management (documenting and approving changes),
incident response (identifying and containing breaches), disaster recovery planning,
and backup strategies (full, incremental, differential). Data destruction methods
include overwriting, degaussing, and physical shredding. Documentation types include
network diagrams, asset inventories, and standard operating procedures (SOPs).
NARRATION: Change management prevents unauthorized changes; always document what
you do and why.

SLIDE 22 | Troubleshooting Methodology
CompTIA defines six troubleshooting steps: identify the problem (question the user,
gather information), establish a theory of probable cause, test the theory, establish
an action plan, implement the solution, verify functionality, and document the
outcome. Always reproduce the problem before fixing it and consider multiple causes.
NARRATION: Follow the six steps in order; documenting the solution helps future
technicians facing the same problem.

SLIDE 23 | Safety Practices
Electrostatic discharge (ESD) can destroy components; use anti-static wrist straps
and mats when working inside computers. Unplug equipment before servicing. High-
voltage components such as CRT monitors and laser printer drums can hold lethal
charges even when unplugged. Use proper lifting techniques for heavy equipment. SDS
(Safety Data Sheets) provide hazard information for chemicals.
NARRATION: ESD is invisible but destructive; always ground yourself before touching
components inside a computer.

SLIDE 24 | Environmental Controls and Disposal
Data centers require proper temperature (68-77 F), humidity (40-60%), and airflow
management using hot-aisle/cold-aisle configurations. HVAC systems and UPS
(uninterruptible power supplies) protect equipment. Dispose of batteries, toner
cartridges, and electronics through proper e-waste recycling programs. Never pour
chemicals down drains; follow local regulations and SDS guidelines.
NARRATION: Batteries and toner cannot go in regular trash; improper disposal is
both an environmental hazard and a legal violation.

SLIDE 25 | Core 1 Exam Tips (220-1101)
Core 1 covers mobile devices, networking, hardware, virtualization, and cloud
computing. It contains 90 questions answered in 90 minutes; the passing score is
675 out of 900. Question types include multiple choice, drag-and-drop, and
performance-based simulations (PBQs). Focus heavily on networking ports, cable
types, connector types, and wireless standards. Practice PBQs using free online
simulators.
NARRATION: Core 1 is hardware-heavy; memorize connector types, cable specs, and
port numbers before exam day.

SLIDE 26 | Core 2 Exam Tips (220-1102)
Core 2 covers operating systems, security, software troubleshooting, and operational
procedures. It also has 90 questions in 90 minutes with a 700 out of 900 passing
score. Study Windows command-line tools, Active Directory basics, encryption types,
and the malware removal process. For security, know the CIA triad (Confidentiality,
Integrity, Availability) and common attack mitigation strategies.
NARRATION: Core 2 is software and security focused; the CIA triad underpins almost
every security question on the exam.

SLIDE 27 | Study Plan and Practice Exams
Create a study schedule over 8-12 weeks using the official CompTIA study guide and
free resources such as Professor Messer's videos. Take practice exams under timed
conditions to simulate the real test. Review every wrong answer and understand why
the correct answer is right. Join study groups and forums such as Reddit's
r/CompTIA. Schedule your exam through Pearson VUE.
NARRATION: Practice exams under real timing are the most effective study tool; review
wrong answers thoroughly rather than just retaking tests.
