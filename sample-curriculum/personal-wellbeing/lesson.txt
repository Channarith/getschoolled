LESSON: Personal Well-being
LANGUAGE: en

SLIDE 1 | What Is Well-being?
Well-being is not simply the absence of illness — it is a dynamic state of flourishing across five interconnected dimensions: physical (body health and energy), mental (cognitive clarity and emotional health), emotional (managing feelings and building positive affect), social (quality of relationships and sense of belonging), and financial (security and freedom from money stress). Research consistently shows these dimensions influence one another, so neglecting one tends to erode the others over time.
NARRATION: Well-being spans five dimensions — physical, mental, emotional, social, and financial — and all five affect each other.

SLIDE 2 | The Science of Happiness: Positive Psychology
Positive psychology, founded by Martin Seligman in the late 1990s, shifted the field from treating mental illness toward studying what makes life worth living. Seligman's PERMA model identifies five pillars of well-being: Positive Emotions, Engagement (flow states), Relationships, Meaning, and Accomplishment. Decades of research show that people who actively cultivate all five report higher life satisfaction, better physical health, and greater resilience than those who focus on achievement or pleasure alone.
NARRATION: Seligman's PERMA model gives you five evidence-based pillars to build a life of genuine well-being.

SLIDE 3 | Understanding Stress
Stress is the body's physiological response to perceived demands that exceed available resources. The stress response — coordinated by the hypothalamus, pituitary, and adrenal glands — releases cortisol and adrenaline, raising heart rate, blood pressure, and blood sugar to prepare the body to fight or flee. Acute stress in short bursts can sharpen focus and performance; the problem arises when this system is chronically activated in response to modern pressures like workload, relationships, and financial worry.
NARRATION: Stress is a biological alarm system designed for short bursts — the problem is when modern life keeps it switched on all day.

SLIDE 4 | Acute vs Chronic Stress
Acute stress is short-lived and often motivating — the tension before a presentation, an urgent deadline, a competitive sports moment. The body recovers quickly, cortisol drops, and no lasting harm is done. Chronic stress means the stress response stays elevated for weeks, months, or years. Research from the American Psychological Association links chronic stress to increased risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, depression, anxiety disorders, gastrointestinal problems, and a suppressed immune system.
NARRATION: Acute stress sharpens performance; chronic stress, sustained over months, damages nearly every system in the body.

SLIDE 5 | Evidence-Based Stress Management
The most robustly researched stress reduction techniques require no equipment and can be practiced anywhere. Diaphragmatic breathing (slow, deep breaths that expand the belly rather than the chest) activates the parasympathetic nervous system and lowers cortisol within minutes. Progressive Muscle Relaxation — tensing and releasing muscle groups from feet to face — reduces physical tension and has strong evidence for treating anxiety and insomnia. Body Scan meditation brings non-judgmental attention to physical sensations from head to toe, interrupting the stress-thought cycle.
NARRATION: Diaphragmatic breathing activates your parasympathetic nervous system and measurably lowers cortisol within just a few minutes.

SLIDE 6 | Mindfulness and Meditation: What the Research Says
Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), developed by Jon Kabat-Zinn at the University of Massachusetts, is an eight-week structured program that has been studied in over a thousand peer-reviewed trials. Consistent practice produces measurable reductions in anxiety, depression, and chronic pain, as well as changes in brain structure — including increased gray matter density in the prefrontal cortex and reduced reactivity in the amygdala. Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT), built on MBSR, reduces the recurrence of major depression by approximately 43% in people with three or more prior episodes.
NARRATION: MBSR is one of the most studied behavioral interventions in medicine, with strong evidence for reducing anxiety, depression, and chronic pain.

SLIDE 7 | Building a Sustainable Sleep Habit
Adults need 7 to 9 hours of sleep per night; fewer than 6 hours for multiple consecutive nights impairs cognitive performance as severely as 24-hour sleep deprivation. Sleep hygiene practices with the strongest evidence include keeping a consistent wake time seven days a week (even weekends), avoiding caffeine after 2 pm, and keeping the bedroom dark, cool (around 18 degrees Celsius), and quiet. Blue-spectrum light from screens suppresses melatonin production — use Night Shift or blue-light-blocking glasses for at least an hour before bed.
NARRATION: A consistent wake time, even on weekends, is the single most impactful sleep hygiene change for most people.

SLIDE 8 | Physical Activity and Mental Health
Exercise is one of the most powerful interventions for mental health available. A landmark 2023 meta-analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine found that physical activity was 1.5 times more effective than medication or counseling for reducing depression and anxiety symptoms. The mechanism involves increased BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), which promotes neuroplasticity, as well as endorphin and serotonin release. Even 30 minutes of moderate-intensity exercise (brisk walking) three times per week produces clinically significant effects.
NARRATION: A 2023 meta-analysis found that exercise is 1.5 times more effective than medication for reducing depression and anxiety.

SLIDE 9 | Nutrition and the Gut-Brain Axis
The gut contains approximately 100 million neurons and produces around 90% of the body's serotonin — making nutrition a direct lever for mental well-being. The Mediterranean diet (rich in vegetables, legumes, whole grains, fish, and olive oil) is the most consistently evidence-backed eating pattern for both brain health and longevity. Anti-inflammatory foods (berries, leafy greens, fatty fish, turmeric) reduce neuroinflammation linked to depression. Ultra-processed foods high in refined sugar are associated with higher rates of anxiety and mood instability across multiple large cohort studies.
NARRATION: The Mediterranean diet is the most evidence-backed eating pattern for brain health, mood stability, and long-term well-being.

SLIDE 10 | Social Connection and Longevity
The Harvard Study of Adult Development, which has followed hundreds of participants for over 80 years, reached a clear conclusion: the quality of close relationships is the strongest predictor of happiness and longevity in midlife and beyond — more than wealth, fame, or social class. Social isolation raises mortality risk by approximately 26%, comparable to smoking 15 cigarettes per day. The mechanism includes both behavioral factors (isolation reduces health behaviors) and direct biological effects of loneliness on cortisol levels and inflammation.
NARRATION: The Harvard Study found that relationship quality predicts longevity more powerfully than wealth, fame, or any other factor studied.

SLIDE 11 | Building and Maintaining Meaningful Relationships
Meaningful relationships require consistent, small investments of time and attention rather than rare grand gestures. Relationship researcher John Gottman found that the ratio of positive to negative interactions in a healthy relationship is approximately 5 to 1 — five moments of warmth, interest, or appreciation for every one moment of conflict or criticism. Practical habits include scheduled weekly check-ins with close friends, actively listening without planning your response, and expressing specific appreciation rather than generic praise.
NARRATION: Gottman's research shows that five positive interactions for every one negative one is the ratio that keeps relationships strong.

SLIDE 12 | The Science of Gratitude
Gratitude journaling — writing three specific things you are grateful for, and why, three to five times per week — produces measurable improvements in mood and life satisfaction within four weeks in randomized controlled trials. The mechanism is cognitive: directing attention to what is working counteracts the negativity bias, a hardwired tendency to register threats and losses more strongly than gains. Brain imaging studies show gratitude activates the medial prefrontal cortex, the brain region associated with learning and decision-making, suggesting it literally rewires neural patterns over time.
NARRATION: Gratitude journaling three times per week has been shown in controlled trials to measurably improve mood within four weeks.

SLIDE 13 | Emotional Regulation Strategies
Cognitive reappraisal — reframing how you interpret a situation — is one of the most effective emotion regulation strategies studied in psychology. For example, viewing a difficult conversation as an opportunity to understand someone rather than a threat reduces both subjective distress and physiological arousal. Opposite Action, a DBT (Dialectical Behaviour Therapy) technique, involves doing the behavioral opposite of what an emotion urges: when anxiety says withdraw, approach instead; when depression says stay in bed, get up and move. These strategies change emotions by changing either cognition or behavior.
NARRATION: Cognitive reappraisal — deliberately reinterpreting a situation — reduces both the feeling of distress and the physical stress response.

SLIDE 14 | TIPP Skills from DBT
The TIPP skills from Dialectical Behaviour Therapy are designed for moments of intense emotional overwhelm: Temperature (holding ice or splashing cold water on your face activates the dive reflex and rapidly calms the nervous system), Intense Exercise (even 20 minutes reduces the physiological intensity of emotion), Paced Breathing (slowing exhales to twice the length of inhales), and Progressive relaxation. These skills work through direct physiological intervention rather than thinking — which is important because in states of high emotion, the thinking brain is less accessible.
NARRATION: Splashing cold water on your face activates the dive reflex and is one of the fastest ways to reduce intense emotional overwhelm.

SLIDE 15 | Growth Mindset vs Fixed Mindset
Carol Dweck's research at Stanford identified two fundamental beliefs people hold about their abilities. A fixed mindset assumes intelligence and talent are innate and static — you either have it or you don't. A growth mindset holds that abilities develop through dedication and learning. People with growth mindsets recover faster from failure, seek more challenging tasks, and achieve more over time. The good news: mindset is not fixed — deliberate reflection on how you respond to difficulty can shift it.
NARRATION: Dweck's research shows that believing abilities can grow with effort leads to more learning, persistence, and achievement over time.

SLIDE 16 | Purpose and Meaning: Ikigai and Simon Sinek's Why
Meaning is one of the strongest predictors of psychological well-being. The Japanese concept of Ikigai — "reason for being" — sits at the intersection of what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs, and what you can be paid for. Simon Sinek's Golden Circle framework (Start with Why) applies similar thinking to professional identity: people who can articulate the purpose behind their work report higher engagement, lower burnout, and greater resilience during setbacks. Both frameworks suggest clarity about meaning is a skill that can be cultivated.
NARRATION: Ikigai maps your well-being sweet spot at the intersection of what you love, do well, can earn from, and what the world needs.

SLIDE 17 | Managing Digital Overwhelm and Screen Time
The average person checks their phone 96 times per day, and each interruption takes an average of 23 minutes to fully regain deep focus. Research links heavy social media use — particularly passive scrolling — with increased anxiety and depression, especially in adolescents and young adults. Practical strategies include phone-free first and last 30 minutes of the day, notification batching (checking email and messages at three fixed times rather than continuously), and using app timers on social platforms to create friction around unconscious scrolling.
NARRATION: Each phone notification takes an average of 23 minutes to fully recover from — batching your checks protects deep work and mental health.

SLIDE 18 | Financial Well-being
Financial stress is one of the leading causes of anxiety and relationship conflict globally, and it has measurable physical health consequences — including elevated blood pressure, poor sleep, and higher rates of depression. Financial well-being is not about income level; it is about the gap between income and spending, the presence of an emergency fund (3 to 6 months of expenses), and freedom from high-interest debt. Even small improvements — automating savings, tracking spending for 30 days, paying off one debt — produce rapid reductions in financial anxiety.
NARRATION: An emergency fund of three to six months of expenses is the single strongest buffer against financial stress and anxiety.

SLIDE 19 | Building Resilience: The ABCDE Model
Martin Seligman's ABCDE model teaches resilience by restructuring how you respond to adversity. Adversity (the trigger), Belief (what you automatically think about it), Consequence (how you feel and act), Disputation (challenging the belief with evidence), and Energization (the positive result of accurate disputation). The critical insight is that it is not the adversity itself that causes distress but the belief you hold about it. Disputing catastrophic or personalizing beliefs with factual counter-evidence interrupts the emotional consequence before it becomes behavior.
NARRATION: In the ABCDE model, it is your belief about adversity, not the adversity itself, that determines how you feel and respond.

SLIDE 20 | Post-Traumatic Growth
Post-traumatic growth (PTG), studied by Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun, refers to positive psychological change that emerges from the struggle with highly challenging life circumstances. It appears in five domains: personal strength, new possibilities, relating to others, appreciation for life, and spiritual or existential change. PTG does not mean the trauma was good or that distress is absent — it means that through processing difficulty with support and reflection, people often discover strengths and values they did not know they had.
NARRATION: Post-traumatic growth shows that some people emerge from serious adversity with deeper strength, relationships, and sense of purpose.

SLIDE 21 | Self-Compassion vs Self-Criticism
Kristin Neff's research at the University of Texas found that self-compassion — treating yourself with the same kindness you would offer a good friend in difficulty — is associated with lower anxiety, depression, and shame, and higher motivation and emotional resilience. Self-compassion has three components: self-kindness (rather than harsh self-judgment), common humanity (recognizing that struggle is universal), and mindfulness (holding painful feelings in balanced awareness rather than suppressing or over-identifying). Crucially, self-compassion does not reduce motivation — it actually increases it by making failure feel survivable.
NARRATION: Neff's research shows that self-compassion, unlike self-criticism, increases resilience and motivation without reducing accountability.

SLIDE 22 | Creating a Personal Well-being Plan
A personal well-being plan applies behavioral science to your own life. Start with a baseline assessment across the five dimensions of well-being and identify the one or two areas with the greatest gap between current state and desired state. Set SMART goals in those areas (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound). Use habit stacking — attaching a new behavior to an existing one (after I pour my morning coffee, I will write one gratitude entry) — to reduce the friction of starting. Build in weekly reflection to track what is working.
NARRATION: Habit stacking attaches a new well-being behavior to an existing routine so you never have to remember to do it separately.

SLIDE 23 | Navigating Grief, Loss, and Life Transitions
Grief is not limited to bereavement — it accompanies any significant loss: a relationship, a job, a health diagnosis, a role or identity. Elisabeth Kubler-Ross's stages (denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance) describe common experiences but are not a linear prescription; people move between them fluidly and return to earlier stages. George Bonanno's resilience research found that most people (roughly 35 to 65% depending on the loss) show natural resilience — sustained function with manageable grief — without formal intervention, which is itself a reassuring finding.
NARRATION: Grief follows no set timeline or sequence — most people recover with natural resilience, but support dramatically speeds the process for those who need it.

SLIDE 24 | When to Seek Professional Help
Knowing when to seek professional support is a sign of self-awareness, not weakness. Consider reaching out to a mental health professional when distress lasts more than two weeks and impairs daily functioning, when you are using substances to cope, when you are having thoughts of self-harm, or when well-being strategies you have tried on your own are not working. Therapy (talking with a psychologist or counselor), psychiatry (medical management of mental health conditions), and coaching (goal-oriented development work) serve different needs — a GP can help navigate which is appropriate.
NARRATION: Reaching out for professional help is a skilled, proactive response to difficulty — not a sign of failure.

SLIDE 25 | Daily Rituals That Sustain Long-Term Well-being
Research on habit formation and behavioral change points consistently toward the power of small, consistent daily practices over occasional large efforts. A morning routine of 10 minutes (hydration, brief movement, and one conscious intention) sets the biological and psychological tone for the day. An evening wind-down (dimming lights, stopping work at a fixed time, brief reflection) signals the nervous system to shift toward rest. Weekly practices — exercise, connection with someone meaningful, time outdoors — compound over months and years into robust well-being that is resilient to setbacks.
NARRATION: Small daily rituals — consistent sleep, brief movement, and one moment of gratitude — compound into robust well-being over months and years.

SLIDE 26 | Putting It All Together
Well-being is not a destination but an ongoing practice of small, evidence-based choices made in the direction of flourishing. No single intervention works for everyone, which is why building a personal toolkit across physical, mental, emotional, social, and financial dimensions gives you options when one strategy is unavailable. The most important predictor of sustained well-being is not any single habit but the belief that your actions matter — the conviction that effort, attention, and care directed toward your own flourishing will, over time, produce a life worth living.
NARRATION: A life of well-being is built one small, intentional choice at a time — start with one area today and build from there.
