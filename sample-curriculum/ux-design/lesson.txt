LESSON: UX Design Fundamentals
LANGUAGE: en

SLIDE 1 | What Is UX Design?
User Experience (UX) design is the practice of shaping how people feel when they interact with a product — ensuring every touchpoint is useful, usable, and delightful. UX design is often confused with UI design (User Interface design), which focuses specifically on the visual layer: colors, typography, and layout. Product design is the broadest umbrella, encompassing UX, UI, and the strategic decisions about what to build and why. A UX designer's core job is to advocate for the user by understanding their needs, behaviors, and frustrations, then translating those insights into product decisions.
NARRATION: UX design shapes how people feel when using a product, going far deeper than visuals to address usefulness, usability, and human behavior.

SLIDE 2 | The UX Design Process: Discover, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test
The UX design process is iterative rather than linear, cycling through five phases that can be revisited as new information emerges. In the Discover phase, designers conduct research to understand users and the problem space without jumping to solutions. The Define phase synthesizes research into clear problem statements and design criteria. Ideation generates a wide range of possible solutions through brainstorming, sketching, and collaborative workshops. Prototyping builds low- to high-fidelity representations of ideas quickly and cheaply, and Testing validates those ideas with real users before any expensive development begins.
NARRATION: The UX process moves through discovery, definition, ideation, prototyping, and testing in repeating cycles that sharpen solutions over time.

SLIDE 3 | User Research Methods
User research is the systematic study of target users to inform design decisions, and choosing the right method depends on what you need to learn. Generative research methods — interviews, diary studies, and contextual inquiry (observing users in their natural environment) — explore unknown problems and motivations before any design work begins. Evaluative methods — usability testing, A/B testing, and surveys — assess whether a design works once you have something to test. Quantitative research (surveys, analytics) tells you what is happening at scale, while qualitative research (interviews, observations) tells you why, making the two approaches most powerful when used together.
NARRATION: Choosing the right research method — generative or evaluative, qualitative or quantitative — determines how useful the insights will be for design decisions.

SLIDE 4 | Writing a Research Plan and Discussion Guide
A research plan documents what you want to learn, why it matters, which method you'll use, who you'll recruit, and how you'll analyze findings — it aligns stakeholders before a single session begins. The study objectives should be framed as questions, not assumptions: "How do users currently manage expense reports?" not "Do users like our expense form?" A discussion guide for a 60-minute interview typically opens with five minutes of rapport-building, spends 40 minutes exploring core topics through open-ended questions, and reserves 10 minutes for follow-up probes and a final catch-all. Pilot testing your discussion guide with one internal participant exposes confusing questions and pacing issues before you involve real users.
NARRATION: A clear research plan and discussion guide keep interviews focused and ensure you come away with findings that actually inform design decisions.

SLIDE 5 | Affinity Mapping and Synthesizing Research
Affinity mapping transforms raw research data — interview quotes, observations, survey responses — into organized clusters of insight that reveal patterns across participants. The process starts by writing each discrete observation on a sticky note (physical or digital in tools like FigJam or Miro), then grouping related notes into themes collaboratively with the team. Each cluster is labeled with an insight statement — not a topic label ("navigation") but an observation with a point of view ("users expect to find account settings under their profile photo"). Synthesis culminates in a findings report or insight statements that bridge raw data and design decisions, making the leap from "what we heard" to "what it means for the product" explicit.
NARRATION: Affinity mapping turns dozens of raw research notes into clear insight clusters that your whole team can act on.

SLIDE 6 | User Personas: Building and Using Them
A user persona is a composite archetype built from real research data that represents a distinct segment of your user base, giving teams a shared reference point when making design decisions. A well-constructed persona includes a name and photo (to humanize), demographic context, primary goals (what they're trying to accomplish), frustrations (what stands in their way), and behavioral patterns (how they currently solve the problem). Personas are distinct from marketing personas in that they focus on behaviors, mental models, and goals rather than demographics and buying power. The test of a good persona is whether it generates useful disagreement: if "Would Maya prefer one-click checkout or a review step?" has a clear answer, your persona is specific enough to be useful.
NARRATION: Personas give your team a shared, research-grounded picture of who you're designing for so decisions don't default to designing for yourselves.

SLIDE 7 | Customer Journey Mapping
A customer journey map visualizes the end-to-end experience a user has with a product or service across time, channels, and emotional states — making invisible friction visible. Journey maps typically include stages (Awareness, Consideration, Purchase, Onboarding, Retention), actions the user takes at each stage, thoughts and questions they have, emotional highs and lows plotted on a curve, and pain points or opportunities for improvement. The most common mistake is creating journey maps in isolation from real research data; a map built from assumptions reflects the business's internal view, not the user's actual experience. Journey maps are most valuable as living documents updated after each round of research, not as one-time deliverables that hang on a wall.
NARRATION: Customer journey maps reveal the emotional highs and lows users experience across every touchpoint, exposing where products fail and where they can delight.

SLIDE 8 | Information Architecture: Organizing Content for Clarity
Information architecture (IA) is the practice of organizing, labeling, and structuring content so users can find what they need without confusion or frustration. Card sorting is a research method where users group labeled cards into categories that make sense to them, revealing how they mentally organize information — open card sorts let users create their own groups, while closed card sorts ask them to sort into predefined categories. Tree testing validates a proposed navigation structure by asking users to find specific items within a text-only hierarchy, measuring findability success rate and time before any visual design is built. A well-structured site map documents the complete page hierarchy, content relationships, and navigation paths, serving as the blueprint that content, design, and development teams all work from.
NARRATION: Card sorting and tree testing let you design navigation around how users actually think, not how your organization is structured internally.

SLIDE 9 | Interaction Design Principles
Interaction design defines how users engage with a system moment-to-moment, and several psychology-based principles guide effective decisions. Fitts's Law states that the time to acquire a target is a function of distance and size — which is why primary action buttons should be large and close to where users' attention naturally falls. Hick's Law shows that decision time increases logarithmally with the number of choices, explaining why reducing options (progressive disclosure, simplified menus) speeds up task completion. Affordances are properties of an object that signal how it should be used — a button looks pressable, a text underline looks clickable — and good interaction design makes affordances immediately obvious. Feedback (visual, audio, haptic) closes the action loop by confirming to users that the system received their input.
NARRATION: Principles like Fitts's Law, Hick's Law, and affordances provide evidence-based guidelines for designing interactions that feel natural and fast.

SLIDE 10 | Wireframing: From Sketches to Digital Wireframes
Wireframes are low-to-mid fidelity representations of a design that communicate layout, hierarchy, and functionality without color, final typography, or realistic imagery — they keep attention on structure rather than aesthetics. The fastest wireframes are hand-drawn sketches on paper or a whiteboard, ideal for rapid ideation and communicating concepts in team discussions where the roughness signals openness to change. Digital wireframes in tools like Figma, Balsamiq, or Whimsical add precision and make it easier to share with remote stakeholders and annotate with interaction notes. A common mistake is jumping straight to high-fidelity mockups; wireframing first ensures layout and flow decisions are validated cheaply before investing time in polished visuals.
NARRATION: Wireframes let you test layouts and user flows quickly and cheaply before committing any time to high-fidelity visual design.

SLIDE 11 | Prototyping Tools: Figma, Sketch, and Adobe XD
Prototyping tools let designers build interactive simulations of a product without writing any code, enabling realistic user testing before development begins. Figma has become the industry standard for most teams due to its browser-based collaboration (multiple designers editing simultaneously), robust component and auto-layout system, and built-in prototyping with transitions, overlays, and smart animations. Sketch remains popular among macOS-focused studios, especially for design system work, and integrates with Abstract for version control and InVision for prototyping. Adobe XD offers deep integration with the Creative Cloud ecosystem (Photoshop, Illustrator assets) and is often preferred by teams already embedded in Adobe workflows. All three tools support design handoff features where developers can inspect component specs, spacing, and CSS values without needing to ask a designer.
NARRATION: Figma's real-time collaboration and component system have made it the dominant prototyping tool, but the best tool is the one your team already uses well.

SLIDE 12 | Design Systems: Components, Tokens, and Grids
A design system is a shared library of reusable components, design tokens, and guidelines that ensures visual and behavioral consistency across every product surface and team. Design tokens are the named variables that store visual decisions — `color-primary-500: #2563EB`, `spacing-4: 16px`, `font-size-body: 16px` — and act as the bridge between design files and code, allowing a single token update to propagate everywhere. A component library (buttons, inputs, modals, cards) built on these tokens means designers never redesign a button and developers never re-implement one; both reference the same source of truth. Maintaining a design system requires governance: a dedicated owner or team, a clear contribution process, versioning, and thorough documentation of when to use each component and why.
NARRATION: Design tokens and component libraries create a single source of truth that keeps design and code consistent at scale without constant coordination overhead.

SLIDE 13 | Accessibility in UX: WCAG 2.1 and Inclusive Design
Accessibility is not an optional feature — it is a legal requirement in many jurisdictions and a moral baseline for any product that claims to serve people. WCAG 2.1 AA is the most widely adopted accessibility standard, organized around four principles: Perceivable (can users perceive all content?), Operable (can they use the interface?), Understandable (is content and behavior clear?), and Robust (does it work with assistive technologies?). Practical requirements include a minimum color contrast ratio of 4.5:1 for body text, all interactive elements being reachable and operable via keyboard, meaningful alt text for images, and form fields being programmatically associated with their labels. Designing for screen readers means testing with VoiceOver (macOS/iOS) and NVDA (Windows) to ensure focus order, ARIA roles, and live region announcements work correctly.
NARRATION: Meeting WCAG 2.1 AA standards ensures your product is usable by people with visual, motor, cognitive, and auditory disabilities — and is often required by law.

SLIDE 14 | Usability Testing: Methods, Tasks, and Recruiting
Usability testing observes real users attempting realistic tasks with your design to identify where they struggle, confused, or fail — revealing problems no internal review can catch. Moderated testing involves a facilitator guiding participants through tasks and asking follow-up questions in real time, providing rich qualitative data and the ability to probe unexpected behavior. Unmoderated remote testing (via tools like UserTesting.com, Maze, or Lookback) recruits participants who complete tasks independently while their screen and audio are recorded, scaling to more sessions at lower cost. Writing good task scenarios is critical: tasks must be realistic ("You want to cancel your subscription — go ahead") not leading ("Click the Cancel button") and should reflect actual user goals without hinting at the solution.
NARRATION: Watching real users attempt realistic tasks is the most reliable way to find usability problems before they reach your entire customer base.

SLIDE 15 | Micro-Interactions and Motion Design
Micro-interactions are the small, single-purpose moments of interactivity that communicate system state, provide feedback, and make a product feel alive and responsive rather than static. Classic examples include the like button animation on Instagram, the pull-to-refresh animation, password strength indicators that update in real time, and loading spinners that give users confidence the system is working. Motion design should serve communication, not decoration — animations that are too fast (under 100ms) go unnoticed, while animations that are too slow (over 500ms) feel sluggish and frustrate users. The Material Design and Apple Human Interface Guidelines both specify easing curves and duration ranges for different animation types, providing evidence-based defaults any designer can adopt.
NARRATION: Micro-interactions and motion design transform static interfaces into responsive, communicative experiences that feel polished and trustworthy.

SLIDE 16 | Responsive and Mobile-First Design
Mobile-first design starts the design process at the smallest screen size and progressively enhances the layout for larger screens, forcing prioritization of the most essential content and functionality. With over 60% of global web traffic coming from mobile devices, a desktop-first approach that then gets "squeezed" onto mobile consistently produces worse mobile experiences. Responsive design uses CSS breakpoints (commonly 320px, 768px, 1024px, 1440px) and fluid grids to reflow layouts based on viewport size, but design decisions — touch target sizes (minimum 44x44px per Apple HIG), thumb zone accessibility, and reduced cognitive load — must be made intentionally in design tools, not left to developers. Testing responsive designs on real devices (not just browser emulators) catches physical ergonomics issues that simulators miss.
NARRATION: Starting design at mobile screen sizes forces you to prioritize ruthlessly and produces better experiences for the majority of users on small screens.

SLIDE 17 | Design Handoff to Developers
Design handoff is the process of transferring design decisions to the engineering team with enough precision and context that developers can implement the design accurately without constant back-and-forth. Figma's Dev Mode (and Inspect panels in Sketch and XD) lets developers click on any element to see exact spacing, font sizes, colors (in hex, RGB, or design token names), and auto-generated CSS, reducing the need for "pixel-perfect" redline documents. Annotations — sticky notes or numbered callouts attached to design frames — document interaction details that visual specs can't convey: hover states, loading states, error messages, animation timing, and edge cases with long text or empty states. A well-executed handoff treats the component library and design system as the primary documentation, with developers referencing component behavior specs rather than needing to infer intent from static mockups.
NARRATION: Thorough handoff documentation and shared design tokens mean developers can implement your designs accurately without needing to interrupt you for clarification.

SLIDE 18 | Design Critique and Feedback Culture
Design critique is a structured practice for giving and receiving specific, actionable feedback on work in progress — distinct from approval-seeking, which often leads to vague "looks good to me" responses that don't improve the design. Effective critique separates observation ("The primary CTA is low contrast against the background") from interpretation ("This might make the action harder to find") from suggestion ("Consider darkening the button color or reversing it out in white") rather than jumping straight to prescriptive solutions. The "I like, I wish, What if" framework from Stanford d.school gives non-designers language to participate constructively in critique without overstepping into design direction. Designers should present work with the design problem and constraints stated upfront so reviewers evaluate against the right criteria rather than personal preference.
NARRATION: Structured critique that separates observation from interpretation from suggestion produces far better feedback than open-ended "what do you think" reviews.

SLIDE 19 | DesignOps and Agile Teams
DesignOps (Design Operations) is the practice of orchestrating people, processes, and tools to enable design teams to work efficiently and at scale within larger organizations. On agile teams, designers typically work one sprint ahead of developers — conducting research and producing designs in the current sprint for the features engineers will build in the next — a pattern called "design in parallel." The main tension in agile design is between the iterative speed agile encourages and the research rigor UX requires; resolving it means doing continuous lightweight research (weekly user calls, session recordings) rather than big-bang discovery phases. Common DesignOps investments include design system maintenance, tooling standardization, onboarding documentation, and establishing shared rituals like weekly design reviews and cross-functional critique sessions.
NARRATION: Working one sprint ahead of engineering and maintaining continuous lightweight research helps UX designers stay in sync with agile development cycles.

SLIDE 20 | A/B Testing and Data-Informed Design
A/B testing in product design presents two versions of an interface to different user segments simultaneously, measuring which version better achieves a defined metric — conversion rate, task completion, engagement — with statistical rigor. A test is only valid when one variable is changed at a time (button color, headline, CTA text), the sample size is large enough to achieve statistical significance (typically 95% confidence), and the test runs long enough to capture weekly behavioral variation. Data informs design but should not replace design judgment: analytics tell you what users do, not why, and optimizing locally for a metric can degrade the overall user experience. The most effective teams use quantitative data to identify where to focus and qualitative research to understand what to change.
NARRATION: A/B testing validates design decisions with real user behavior data, but qualitative research is still needed to understand the why behind the numbers.

SLIDE 21 | UX Portfolio: Case Study Structure
A UX portfolio demonstrates your design process and thinking, not just finished visuals — hiring managers want to see how you approach problems, not just what the final product looked like. Each case study should follow a narrative arc: the problem and who it affected, your research process and what you discovered, how insights informed your design decisions, the iterations you made and why, and the outcomes or impact (user satisfaction scores, conversion lift, task completion improvement). Three to five well-documented case studies beat ten shallow ones; include your role clearly so reviewers understand what you personally contributed versus what the team did. Real-world work is always preferred over class projects, but a well-documented personal project or volunteer redesign that shows rigorous process is far more compelling than a polished portfolio with no explanation of reasoning.
NARRATION: Strong UX case studies document your thinking process, not just your outputs — showing why you made decisions matters more than showing what the final design looked like.

SLIDE 22 | The Double Diamond and Design Thinking
The Double Diamond model, developed by the British Design Council, visualizes the design process as two divergent-convergent phases: the first diamond discovers and defines the right problem, the second diamond develops and delivers the right solution. Diverging means opening up thinking — gathering broad research, exploring many possible problem framings, generating many solution ideas — before converging to a focused decision. Design thinking, popularized by IDEO and Stanford d.school, describes a similar mindset: Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test, with the emphasis on building genuine empathy for users before jumping to solutions. The most common failure in product development is skipping the first diamond entirely — jumping to solutions for a problem that hasn't been properly understood — which is why divergent problem exploration is a discipline in itself.
NARRATION: The Double Diamond reminds us to fully explore the problem space before committing to solutions — skipping this step is the most expensive mistake in product development.

SLIDE 23 | UX Writing and Content Design
UX writing is the practice of crafting every word users encounter in a product — button labels, error messages, onboarding tooltips, empty states, and confirmation dialogs — to be clear, concise, and useful at the exact moment of need. Bad UX writing creates confusion: a button labeled "Submit" tells users nothing about what will happen, while "Send My Application" sets clear expectations and reduces anxiety. Error messages should tell users what went wrong, why it matters, and what to do next — "Something went wrong" is useless while "Your session expired. Please log in again to continue." is actionable. Content design goes further, ensuring that the information architecture, content hierarchy, and language all work together to guide users to successful outcomes, not just providing accurate copy.
NARRATION: Every word in your product interface is a UX decision — clear, action-oriented language reduces confusion and builds trust at every step of the user journey.

SLIDE 24 | Ethics in Design: Dark Patterns and Inclusive Design
Dark patterns are UI/UX designs that intentionally deceive or manipulate users into actions they didn't intend — subscribing to a service, sharing more data than necessary, or making cancellation deliberately difficult. Examples include disguised ads (ads styled to look like organic content), misdirection (drawing attention away from the opt-out option), confirmshaming ("No thanks, I don't want to save money"), and roach motel patterns (easy to get in, hard to get out, as with subscription cancellations). Inclusive design goes beyond accessibility standards to intentionally include people who are often excluded from mainstream design processes: people with cognitive differences, low digital literacy, non-native language speakers, and people in low-bandwidth environments. Ethical design practice involves asking "could this design harm someone?" at every stage and having the organizational courage to push back on business requests that exploit users.
NARRATION: Ethical designers recognize dark patterns, refuse to implement manipulative UI, and proactively design for users who are often excluded from mainstream product thinking.

SLIDE 25 | Career Paths in UX Design
UX design encompasses a range of specialized roles that offer distinct career trajectories depending on your strengths and interests. User researchers focus exclusively on planning and conducting studies, synthesizing findings, and building organizational research practices; senior researchers often become research operations leads or heads of research. Interaction designers (IxD) specialize in defining behaviors, flows, and micro-interactions rather than visual design, working closely with engineering on how the product feels in motion. UX managers and design directors lead teams, define design strategy, manage stakeholder relationships, and represent design at the executive level. The most valuable career investment early in UX is breadth — developing working competence in research, interaction design, visual design, and writing — before specializing, because cross-disciplinary fluency makes you a far more effective collaborator and leader.
NARRATION: UX careers branch into research, interaction design, content design, and leadership — broad early experience across disciplines makes you a more effective specialist later.
