LESSON: Security Guard Certification Training
LANGUAGE: en
AUDIENCE: corporate
TRACK: Privacy
LEVEL: Certification prep
ROLE: Security & facilities
DELIVERY: 2–3 hr live class
FIT: Security officers and site teams responsible for people and property.
SUMMARY: Officer duties, observation, use-of-force basics, and workplace violence awareness.

SLIDE 1 | Role and Responsibilities of a Security Officer
Security officers protect people, property, and information from theft, vandalism,
unauthorized access, and other threats. Duties vary by assignment but typically include
access control, patrol, incident response, report writing, and customer service.
Security officers are the first line of defense at many facilities; they observe and
report, deter criminal activity through presence, and coordinate with law enforcement
when necessary.
NARRATION: The security officer's primary tools are observation, communication, and
documentation; physical force is the last resort, not the first.

SLIDE 2 | Legal Authority and Limits
Security officers are private citizens and do not have police authority. Your legal
authority is the same as any private citizen, extended by the authority granted by
the property owner. You may detain someone only if you have reasonable belief they
have committed a crime on the property (citizen's arrest in most states), and only
for a reasonable time until police arrive. You cannot search a person without consent,
a warrant, or as a condition of entry stated in posted policy. Exceeding legal authority
creates civil and criminal liability.
NARRATION: Acting outside your legal authority exposes you and your employer to
lawsuits; when in doubt, call law enforcement rather than escalate.

SLIDE 3 | Peace Officer vs Security Officer
Peace officers (police) have arrest authority, power to carry weapons under law
enforcement authority, and investigative powers granted by government. Security
officers are private employees with no government-granted authority beyond that of
any citizen. Some states license armed security officers with expanded authority;
requirements vary widely. Never represent yourself as a police officer; impersonating
a peace officer is a criminal offense.
NARRATION: The distinction between security officer and peace officer matters legally;
always be clear about your role to the public and never blur the line.

SLIDE 4 | Trespass Law and Property Rights
Property owners can legally exclude individuals from their private property. A person
who remains on private property after being asked to leave is trespassing. Security
officers may order a trespasser to leave, and if they refuse, may contact police and
request removal. Document all trespass incidents thoroughly, including time, location,
description of the individual, and what was said. Trespass notices (bar letters) are
enforceable legal documents; know how your employer issues them.
NARRATION: The legal power to exclude people from private property is the foundation
of most security work; document every trespass incident in case law enforcement or
court action follows.

SLIDE 5 | Use of Force Continuum
The use of force continuum describes escalating levels of force used in response to
a subject's behavior. Levels typically include: officer presence (most common), verbal
commands, soft empty-hand control (guiding, holding), hard empty-hand control (strikes),
intermediate weapons (baton, OC spray if authorized), and deadly force (only when life
is in immediate danger). Always use the minimum force necessary and de-escalate as
quickly as possible. Document any use of force in detail.
NARRATION: The continuum is a guide, not a rigid ladder; jump to a higher level if the
threat requires it, but always aim to de-escalate as soon as it is safe to do so.

SLIDE 6 | Conflict De-escalation Techniques
De-escalation prevents the need for force by reducing tension. Key techniques: maintain
a calm, non-threatening tone and body language; use the person's name if known; listen
actively and acknowledge their feelings; give the person choices and time to comply;
avoid arguing, interrupting, or issuing ultimatums that back them into a corner; keep
a safe distance (at least arm's length plus the reach of a weapon). Most situations
can be resolved through communication before physical intervention is needed.
NARRATION: People in crisis often respond to calmness with calmness; your tone and
body language can either defuse or escalate a situation.

SLIDE 7 | Emergency Response Procedures - Fire
When a fire is discovered: activate the nearest fire alarm pull station, call 911, and
announce the emergency over the PA system or radio. Assist with building evacuation
using the facility's emergency action plan. Never use elevators during a fire. Direct
occupants to the nearest safe exit and assembly point. Only use a fire extinguisher on
a small, contained fire if you are trained and have an exit behind you. Report to the
incident commander upon arrival of fire department. Account for all personnel.
NARRATION: Your priority in a fire is life safety, not property; get people out first
and account for everyone at the assembly point.

SLIDE 8 | Emergency Response Procedures - Medical
For a medical emergency: call 911 immediately. Provide first aid within your training
and certification level. Retrieve and activate the AED for cardiac arrest victims.
Maintain scene security and keep bystanders clear to allow responders access. Meet
emergency vehicles at the facility entrance to guide them to the patient. Document
time of incident, time 911 was called, and actions taken. Notify your supervisor and
complete an incident report. Preserve any evidence if the cause of injury may be
criminal.
NARRATION: Getting EMS on the way quickly is your first action; every minute of delay
in cardiac arrest significantly reduces the patient's chance of survival.

SLIDE 9 | Active Threat Response
An active threat (such as an active shooter) requires immediate action. The preferred
response model is Run-Hide-Fight: Run and evacuate if there is a safe escape route;
Hide if evacuation is not possible (secure the space, silence phones, turn off lights);
Fight only as a last resort using improvised weapons. Security officers must balance
personal safety with duty. Follow your facility's specific active threat plan. When
law enforcement arrives, show empty hands, follow all commands, and do not block or
guide officers; let them move to the threat.
NARRATION: When police arrive during an active threat, they will treat everyone as a
potential suspect until the scene is secured; hands visible, no sudden movements.

SLIDE 10 | Patrol Techniques - Fixed Post
A fixed post security officer is assigned to a specific location such as a lobby,
gate, or checkpoint. Responsibilities include controlling entry and exit, verifying
credentials, monitoring surveillance cameras, and communicating with mobile units.
Fixed post officers must remain alert despite the monotony of stationary duty. Regular
communication with dispatch and roving officers maintains situational awareness.
Know your post orders (standing post instructions) and follow them precisely.
NARRATION: Post orders are your written instructions for your assignment; read and
understand them before every shift because they contain site-specific rules that
override general training.

SLIDE 11 | Patrol Techniques - Mobile and Foot Patrol
Mobile patrols cover large areas by vehicle, checking multiple locations on a
randomized schedule to avoid predictable patterns. Foot patrols allow closer inspection
of buildings, lots, and perimeter fencing. Check for unlocked doors, broken windows,
graffiti, suspicious vehicles, and people who do not belong. Use the OODA loop
(Observe, Orient, Decide, Act) to process your surroundings continuously. Document
patrol observations in your log, noting time, location, and anything unusual.
NARRATION: Randomizing your patrol route removes the predictability that allows
criminals to time their activities around your schedule.

SLIDE 12 | Access Control Systems
Access control restricts entry to authorized individuals. Systems range from simple
key locks to electronic badge readers, biometric scanners (fingerprint, iris, facial
recognition), and video intercom systems. Tailgating occurs when an unauthorized
person follows an authorized person through a secured door; challenge any individual
who does not badge in. Visitor management systems issue temporary credentials and
require identification. Maintain access control logs for all personnel entries.
NARRATION: Tailgating is one of the most common ways unauthorized people enter secure
facilities; never assume someone who follows a badged employee is authorized.

SLIDE 13 | CCTV and Surveillance Basics
Closed-circuit television (CCTV) provides visual monitoring of areas. Security officers
must know camera locations and coverage gaps. Monitor screens actively; passive
watching allows incidents to go unnoticed. Record and preserve footage of incidents
before it is overwritten. PTZ (pan-tilt-zoom) cameras allow remote control. Camera
placement best practices: cover all entry and exit points, parking areas, cash handling
areas, and high-risk locations. Inform the public that cameras are in use through
posted signage where legally required.
NARRATION: Cameras deter crime and document incidents, but only if someone is actively
monitoring them and preserving footage when needed.

SLIDE 14 | Report Writing and Documentation
Security incident reports are legal documents. All reports must be: factual (only what
you observed, heard, or did), accurate (correct names, times, and locations), clear
and concise (no jargon or vague language), complete (who, what, when, where, how),
and objective (no opinions or conclusions, just facts). Write reports immediately while
details are fresh. Reports may be subpoenaed in criminal or civil proceedings. Errors
in reports can undermine prosecutions and expose employers to liability.
NARRATION: Stick to facts in your report; write what you saw and did, not what you
think happened or why.

SLIDE 15 | Testifying in Court
Security officers may be required to testify about incidents they observed or responded
to. Review your reports and notes before court. Testify only to what you personally
observed; do not speculate or guess. If you do not know or do not remember, say so.
Speak clearly and directly to the judge or jury. Do not argue with attorneys; answer
the question asked and stop talking. Professional appearance and demeanor in court
reflect on your credibility and your employer.
NARRATION: Your credibility in court depends on the accuracy of your report and your
demeanor on the stand; honest, clear testimony is always best.

SLIDE 16 | Radio Communication Protocols
Professional radio communication is clear, brief, and follows established protocols.
Identify yourself and your unit, state the recipient, then give your message. Use plain
language unless your agency uses a standardized 10-code system. Common codes:
10-4 (message received), 10-20 (location), 10-99 (assignment completed). Always confirm
important messages by repeating them back. Keep transmissions under 30 seconds. Use
channel discipline; do not tie up the channel with unnecessary chatter.
NARRATION: Clear radio communication can be the difference between a coordinated
response and chaos during an emergency; practice brevity and clarity on every call.

SLIDE 17 | Handling Difficult Individuals
Difficult situations include angry customers, intoxicated individuals, emotionally
disturbed persons, and people in mental health crises. De-escalation is the primary
tool. Give clear, simple instructions and wait for compliance before escalating. For
intoxicated persons, do not leave them alone if they are in danger; call EMS if needed.
For mental health crises, avoid argumentation, maintain calm, and involve mental health
resources or law enforcement with crisis intervention training (CIT) if available.
Document all interactions.
NARRATION: Intoxicated and mentally ill individuals are often scared or confused; a
calm, patient, and non-threatening approach is almost always more effective than
escalation.

SLIDE 18 | First Aid for Security Officers
Security officers often arrive at medical emergencies before EMS. Core skills include
CPR and AED use, bleeding control with direct pressure and tourniquets, choking
response, and recognizing signs of stroke, heart attack, and overdose. The opioid
antidote naloxone (Narcan) is increasingly carried by security staff; administer per
your training and jurisdiction. Always document first aid provided in the incident
report and transfer care to EMS with a clear verbal handoff of what you observed and
did.
NARRATION: Security officers are often the first responders on scene; basic first aid
skills and CPR certification can mean the difference between life and death.

SLIDE 19 | Cyber and Information Security Basics
Physical security and cyber security intersect. Tailgating into server rooms is a
physical security threat with cyber consequences. Social engineering attempts may target
security personnel for access or information. Sensitive documents must be shredded, not
discarded in open waste bins. Visitors must not be left unattended in secure areas.
Report suspicious behavior such as people photographing secure areas or attempting to
access restricted terminals. Follow your employer's information security policies.
NARRATION: A criminal who gains physical access to a data center can steal or destroy
more data in minutes than a remote hacker can in days; physical and cyber security
must work together.

SLIDE 20 | Privacy and Confidentiality
Security officers routinely handle confidential information including access logs,
incident reports, personnel records, and client information. Never share this information
outside of official reporting channels. Do not discuss incidents on social media or
with friends and family. Confidentiality agreements (NDAs) are common in the industry,
particularly in corporate, healthcare, and government security roles. Breaches of
confidentiality can result in termination, civil liability, and criminal charges.
NARRATION: What you see and hear on duty stays on duty; confidentiality is a condition
of employment in virtually every security role.

SLIDE 21 | Professional Appearance and Conduct
A security officer's appearance communicates authority and professionalism. Maintain
a clean, pressed uniform with all required insignia in place. Proper grooming, polished
boots, and appropriate badge display are standard requirements. Avoid unprofessional
behavior including using personal phones on post, eating or drinking in unauthorized
areas, sleeping on duty, or making personal calls. Conduct on and off duty reflects
on your employer; a security officer with a reputation for integrity commands more
respect and cooperation.
NARRATION: Professional appearance deters crime through visible authority and builds
trust with the public and clients; a disheveled officer undermines both.

SLIDE 22 | Report Writing Practice - Use of Force
Use of force reports require specific documentation: exact time and location, the
subject's behavior that prompted force, each action taken and why, injuries to any
party, witnesses present, and whether medical attention was requested or provided.
These reports are reviewed by supervisors, legal counsel, and potentially courts.
Follow the chronological sequence of events precisely. Do not minimize or exaggerate;
an accurate account is your best legal protection.
NARRATION: Use of force reports are the most legally sensitive documents you will
write; complete them before leaving your shift while every detail is fresh.

SLIDE 23 | State Licensing Requirements Overview
Security guard licensing is regulated at the state level, not federally. Most states
require applicants to be at least 18 years old, pass a criminal background check, and
complete a state-approved training curriculum (typically 8-40+ hours). Armed security
officers face additional requirements including firearm training, range qualification,
and additional background checks. Licenses must be renewed periodically with continuing
education. Some states require you to register with the state before beginning work.
NARRATION: Security guard license requirements vary significantly by state; verify your
state's current requirements through the state licensing board before seeking employment.

SLIDE 24 | Workplace Violence Prevention
Security officers play a key role in identifying and responding to workplace violence
indicators. Warning signs include threatening statements or behavior, sudden changes
in mood or demeanor, obsessive focus on a coworker or supervisor, and bringing weapons
or unusual items to the workplace. Report warning signs to supervisors immediately;
never confront a potentially violent individual alone. Participate in workplace violence
prevention training. Follow the facility's threat management process for reporting
and escalating concerns.
NARRATION: Most workplace violence incidents are preceded by observable warning signs;
early reporting and intervention prevent escalation.

SLIDE 25 | Crowd Management and Event Security
Event security requires planning, teamwork, and communication. Key responsibilities
include ingress and egress control, prohibited item screening, crowd flow management,
locating and assisting distressed individuals, and coordinating with local law
enforcement. Crowd crush (compressive asphyxia) can occur in dense crowds; monitor
crowd density and pressure-wave movement. Establish clear communication channels
with event management. Know emergency evacuation procedures specific to the venue
and the event.
NARRATION: Crowd crush is a silent danger; people can be asphyxiated while still
standing without room to fall; monitor density and open emergency corridors early.

SLIDE 26 | Career Development in Security
Entry-level security officers can advance to supervisory, investigative, loss prevention,
and management roles. Specialized certifications include the Certified Protection
Professional (CPP) from ASIS International, the Physical Security Professional (PSP),
and the Professional Certified Investigator (PCI). Higher-level positions often require
a degree in criminal justice or security management. Building a reputation for
reliability, professional report writing, and sound judgment is the fastest path to
advancement in the security industry.
NARRATION: The CPP from ASIS International is the most respected advanced credential
in the security industry; it signals expert-level knowledge of security management
principles.
