AUDIENCE: corporate

ROLE: All employees

SUMMARY: CIA triad, phishing, passwords, MFA, and everyday cyber hygiene.

SLIDE 1 | The CIA Triad: Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability
The CIA triad is the foundational model of information security, representing three core objectives that every security control, policy, and technology is designed to protect. Confidentiality ensures that information is accessible only to those authorized to see it — encryption, access controls, and classification policies all serve this goal. Integrity ensures that data remains accurate and unaltered except through authorized processes — checksums, digital signatures, and audit logs detect and prevent unauthorized modifications. Availability ensures that systems and data are accessible to authorized users when needed — redundancy, backups, and DDoS mitigation protect this property.
NARRATION: Every security decision maps back to the CIA triad: protecting the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information.

SLIDE 2 | Types of Cyber Threats: Malware
Malware is any malicious software designed to damage, disrupt, or gain unauthorized access to a system, and it comes in several distinct forms with different objectives. Viruses attach themselves to legitimate programs and replicate when the host program runs, spreading to other files or systems. Worms are self-replicating and spread across networks without user interaction, as the 2017 WannaCry worm demonstrated by infecting 200,000 systems across 150 countries in 48 hours. Ransomware encrypts a victim's files and demands payment for the decryption key — the average ransomware payment in 2023 exceeded $1.5 million — while spyware silently records user activity, keystrokes, and credentials without the user's knowledge.
NARRATION: Malware includes viruses, worms, ransomware, and spyware, each with different spreading mechanisms and goals but all designed to compromise systems.

SLIDE 3 | Phishing, Social Engineering, and Insider Threats
Social engineering attacks exploit human psychology rather than technical vulnerabilities, making them effective against even well-patched systems and accounting for over 90% of successful cyberattacks. Phishing emails impersonate trusted senders to trick recipients into clicking malicious links, downloading attachments, or entering credentials on fake websites; spear phishing targets specific individuals with personalized details that make the deception far more convincing. Vishing (voice phishing) and smishing (SMS phishing) extend these tactics to phone calls and text messages, often impersonating IT support, banks, or government agencies. Insider threats — employees, contractors, or partners who misuse legitimate access — are particularly damaging because they bypass perimeter controls entirely; they may be malicious (data theft, sabotage) or accidental (misconfiguring a system, clicking a phishing link).
NARRATION: Social engineering exploits human trust rather than software vulnerabilities, which is why security awareness training is as important as technical controls.

SLIDE 4 | The Cyber Kill Chain: Understanding Attack Stages
The Cyber Kill Chain, developed by Lockheed Martin, describes the seven stages of a targeted cyberattack from initial reconnaissance to achieving the attacker's objective — understanding this model helps defenders identify and break attacks at the earliest possible stage. Reconnaissance involves the attacker gathering intelligence about the target: scanning for open ports, researching employees on LinkedIn, and identifying technologies used. Weaponization pairs an exploit with a delivery mechanism (a malicious PDF or macro-enabled Word document). Delivery transmits the weapon — via phishing email, compromised website, or USB drive. Exploitation executes the payload on the victim's system, followed by Installation of persistent malware. Command and Control (C2) establishes a covert communication channel back to the attacker, and Actions on Objectives is where the attacker achieves their goal: data exfiltration, ransomware deployment, or lateral movement to higher-value targets.
NARRATION: Breaking the Kill Chain at any stage — by blocking delivery, detecting installation, or disrupting C2 — stops an attack before the attacker achieves their objective.

SLIDE 5 | Network Fundamentals for Security
Understanding networking fundamentals is prerequisite knowledge for nearly every area of cybersecurity, as attacks travel through networks and most defenses are deployed at network boundaries. The OSI model describes communication in seven layers: Physical, Data Link, Network, Transport, Session, Presentation, and Application — attacks and defenses operate at specific layers, so knowing the model helps you understand where a vulnerability or control lives. The TCP/IP suite is the practical implementation: IP handles addressing and routing, TCP provides reliable delivery with handshakes, and UDP provides fast connectionless delivery used by DNS and streaming. Firewalls filter traffic by IP address, port, and protocol; Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) identify known attack signatures in traffic; Intrusion Prevention Systems (IPS) actively block detected threats in real time.
NARRATION: The OSI model, TCP/IP, and the role of firewalls and IDS/IPS are the foundational networking concepts every security professional must understand.

SLIDE 6 | Network Scanning and Enumeration with Nmap
Network scanning is the process of discovering hosts, open ports, and running services on a network — a technique used both by attackers in reconnaissance and by defenders in security audits and vulnerability assessments. Nmap (Network Mapper) is the most widely used open-source network scanner, capable of host discovery, port scanning, service version detection, and OS fingerprinting. A basic Nmap scan (`nmap -sV 192.168.1.0/24`) scans an entire subnet and identifies which services are running on open ports — for example, finding port 22 (SSH) open tells you SSH is accessible, and the version number may reveal known vulnerabilities. Running Nmap against systems you own or have written permission to scan is legal and encouraged; running it against systems without authorization is a criminal offense in most jurisdictions.
NARRATION: Nmap lets you see your network the way an attacker does — discovering open ports and services before they do is a critical defensive practice.

SLIDE 7 | Authentication, Access Control, and Zero Trust
Authentication verifies who you are; authorization determines what you're permitted to do — and most breaches involve failures of one or both. Multi-factor authentication (MFA) requires at least two of three factors: something you know (password), something you have (authenticator app or hardware token), and something you are (biometric) — enabling MFA blocks over 99% of automated account attacks. Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) grants permissions based on job function rather than individual identity, and the Principle of Least Privilege limits each user and system to only the access needed to perform their function — nothing more. Zero Trust is a security architecture that assumes no user or device inside or outside the network is inherently trusted; every access request is authenticated, authorized, and continuously validated regardless of location.
NARRATION: Multi-factor authentication and least privilege access are two of the highest-impact, lowest-cost security controls any organization can implement immediately.

SLIDE 8 | Password Security: Hashing, Salting, and Credential Hygiene
Passwords are rarely stored as plaintext in modern systems; instead, they are hashed — run through a one-way mathematical function — and only the hash value is stored. SHA-256 is a cryptographic hash function, but it is too fast for password storage because attackers can attempt billions of combinations per second using GPUs; bcrypt, Argon2, and scrypt are purpose-built password hashing algorithms that are intentionally slow, making brute-force attacks impractical. Salting adds a unique random value to each password before hashing, ensuring that two users with the same password produce different hashes and preventing attackers from using precomputed rainbow tables. Credential stuffing attacks use lists of username-password pairs leaked from other breaches to attempt logins at scale — reusing passwords across sites is directly what makes these attacks devastatingly effective, which is why password managers are a security necessity, not a convenience.
NARRATION: Slow hashing algorithms like bcrypt combined with unique salts protect stored passwords, but users must avoid reusing passwords to prevent credential stuffing attacks.

SLIDE 9 | Encryption Fundamentals: Symmetric, Asymmetric, and TLS
Encryption transforms readable plaintext into ciphertext that can only be decoded with the correct key, protecting data confidentiality in storage and in transit. Symmetric encryption (AES-256 is the standard) uses a single key for both encryption and decryption — it is fast and used for bulk data, but requires a secure way to share the key. Asymmetric encryption (RSA, ECC) uses a mathematically linked key pair: a public key encrypts data, and only the corresponding private key can decrypt it — this solves the key exchange problem and is used in TLS, email signing, and code signing. TLS (Transport Layer Security) secures the vast majority of internet traffic by combining asymmetric cryptography for key exchange, symmetric encryption for session data, and digital certificates issued by Certificate Authorities to verify the identity of servers — this is what makes HTTPS trustworthy.
NARRATION: TLS combines asymmetric key exchange with symmetric encryption to protect internet communications, and understanding this handshake explains why HTTPS matters.

SLIDE 10 | Web Application Security: OWASP Top 10
The OWASP Top 10 is the most widely referenced list of critical web application security risks, updated periodically based on data from thousands of real applications and used by developers and security teams worldwide as a baseline for secure development. SQL injection — inserting malicious SQL into an input field to manipulate a database query — remains the most well-known injection attack; parameterized queries and prepared statements eliminate this vulnerability entirely. Cross-Site Scripting (XSS) injects malicious scripts into web pages viewed by other users, enabling session hijacking and data theft; output encoding and Content Security Policy headers prevent it. Broken Access Control, the top-ranked risk in OWASP 2021, occurs when users can access resources or functions beyond their authorization — Insecure Direct Object References (IDOR) are a common manifestation, where changing a URL parameter (order_id=1234 to order_id=1235) exposes another user's data.
NARRATION: The OWASP Top 10 covers the most common web vulnerabilities — injection, broken authentication, XSS, and IDOR — that every developer and security professional must know.

SLIDE 11 | Vulnerability Scanning and Penetration Testing
Vulnerability scanning uses automated tools to identify known vulnerabilities in systems, applications, and network devices by comparing their configurations and software versions against databases of known issues. Tools like Nessus, Qualys, and OpenVAS perform credentialed scans (using valid credentials to examine systems from the inside) and unauthenticated scans (simulating an external attacker's view) and produce prioritized reports. Penetration testing goes further: a skilled ethical hacker actively attempts to exploit vulnerabilities to demonstrate real-world impact, using a structured methodology — reconnaissance, scanning, exploitation, post-exploitation, and reporting. Penetration testing follows a defined scope and rules of engagement documented in a Statement of Work; acting outside that scope without authorization is illegal even for professional penetration testers.
NARRATION: Vulnerability scanning finds known weaknesses automatically, while penetration testing demonstrates what an attacker could actually do with those weaknesses.

SLIDE 12 | Incident Response Lifecycle
The incident response (IR) lifecycle provides a structured process for detecting, containing, and recovering from security incidents — and organizations with a documented IR plan reduce the average cost of a breach by $2.66 million compared to those without one. Preparation includes building the IR team, defining roles and playbooks, and ensuring logging and monitoring are in place before an incident occurs. Detection and Analysis identifies that an incident is occurring and determines its scope, often using SIEM alerts, endpoint detection tools, or threat intelligence feeds. Containment stops the spread — isolating affected systems from the network — while Eradication removes the attacker's presence and the vulnerability they exploited. Recovery restores systems to normal operations from clean backups, and Post-Incident Review (the "lessons learned" phase) documents what happened, what worked, and what to improve — the phase most organizations skip and most consistently regret skipping.
NARRATION: The incident response lifecycle ensures security incidents are handled systematically, minimizing damage and generating the lessons needed to prevent recurrence.

SLIDE 13 | Security Information and Event Management (SIEM)
A SIEM platform aggregates log data from across an entire IT environment — firewalls, servers, endpoints, cloud services, applications — correlates events across sources, and alerts on patterns that indicate security threats, giving security teams a single pane of glass for threat detection. SIEM tools use rules and signatures to detect known attack patterns (a user logging in from two countries simultaneously, a spike in failed authentication attempts) and increasingly use machine learning to establish behavioral baselines and surface anomalies. Splunk, Microsoft Sentinel, IBM QRadar, and Elastic SIEM are the major platforms; cloud-native SIEMs like Sentinel have reduced the infrastructure overhead that made traditional SIEM deployment expensive and complex. The quality of a SIEM deployment depends entirely on the quality and completeness of log ingestion — if an endpoint isn't sending logs, it's a blind spot.
NARRATION: A SIEM correlates logs from every system in your environment to detect attack patterns that no single log source would reveal on its own.

SLIDE 14 | Cloud Security: Shared Responsibility Model
Cloud security operates under a Shared Responsibility Model that divides security obligations between the cloud provider and the customer — and misunderstanding this division is the cause of most cloud security breaches. AWS, Azure, and GCP are responsible for security "of" the cloud: the physical infrastructure, hypervisor, and foundational services are the provider's domain. Customers are responsible for security "in" the cloud: configuring services correctly, managing identities and access, encrypting data, and securing their applications. The most common cloud misconfigurations — publicly exposed S3 buckets, overly permissive IAM roles, unencrypted databases — are entirely the customer's responsibility and are not fixed by the cloud provider. Cloud Security Posture Management (CSPM) tools like AWS Security Hub, Azure Defender, and Wiz continuously scan cloud environments for misconfigurations against security benchmarks.
NARRATION: The Shared Responsibility Model makes clear that cloud providers secure the infrastructure while customers are responsible for how they configure and use it.

SLIDE 15 | Identity and Access Management in Cloud Environments
Cloud IAM controls who can do what within your cloud environment, and poorly configured IAM is consistently the root cause of the most damaging cloud breaches. AWS IAM, Azure Active Directory, and GCP IAM all use policy-based access control where permissions are attached to identities (users, groups, roles) or resources; the safest practice is to grant only the permissions explicitly needed for a task — not the AWS AdministratorAccess policy for a developer who only needs to read S3 buckets. Service accounts and machine identities (used by applications to call APIs) are frequently over-privileged and under-monitored, creating a large and often invisible attack surface. CloudTrail (AWS), Azure Monitor, and GCP Cloud Audit Logs record every API call in your environment, providing the audit trail needed to investigate incidents and detect unauthorized access.
NARRATION: Cloud IAM misconfigurations — over-privileged roles and exposed service account keys — are the most common path attackers use to breach cloud environments.

SLIDE 16 | Endpoint Security: EDR, Patching, and Hardening
Endpoints — laptops, desktops, servers, and mobile devices — are the most common initial access points in cyberattacks because they run user-controlled software and are exposed to phishing, malicious downloads, and removable media. Traditional antivirus uses signature-based detection (matching against known malware samples) and is ineffective against novel threats; Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR) tools like CrowdStrike Falcon, Microsoft Defender for Endpoint, and SentinelOne use behavioral analysis and machine learning to detect suspicious activity in real time and enable rapid investigation and containment. Patch management — the systematic process of identifying, testing, and applying software updates — is non-negotiable: the 2017 Equifax breach that exposed 147 million people's data exploited a vulnerability for which a patch had been available for two months. System hardening reduces the attack surface by disabling unnecessary services, removing default credentials, and configuring systems to the minimum functionality required.
NARRATION: EDR tools detect novel malware through behavioral analysis, but consistent patch management remains the single most effective way to block the majority of attacks.

SLIDE 17 | Social Engineering Attacks: Recognition and Defense
Social engineering succeeds not because users are unintelligent but because attackers invest time in crafting scenarios that are psychologically compelling and contextually plausible. Common manipulation tactics include creating urgency ("Your account will be locked in 30 minutes"), establishing authority ("This is IT support — we need your credentials to fix your account"), inducing fear ("Your computer is infected and must be cleaned immediately"), and building rapport through pretexting — establishing a fake but believable identity over multiple interactions before making the harmful request. Recognizing social engineering requires questioning requests that bypass normal processes, verifying identity through a separate known channel (not the contact information provided by the caller), and understanding that legitimate IT departments never need to ask for your password. The "stop, think, call" framework — pausing before acting, evaluating the request, and verifying through an official channel — stops the majority of social engineering attempts.
NARRATION: Social engineering attacks succeed through urgency, authority, and fear — recognizing these psychological triggers and pausing to verify is your most effective defense.

SLIDE 18 | Security Awareness Training Programs
Security awareness training transforms employees from the most common vulnerability into a functioning layer of the security defense — organizations with ongoing training programs reduce successful phishing attacks by up to 70%. Effective programs move beyond annual compliance training (which improves knowledge but not behavior) to continuous, context-aware micro-training that delivers short lessons immediately after a risky behavior is detected — such as a simulated phishing test that delivers training to users who clicked the link. Phishing simulation platforms like KnowBe4, Proofpoint Security Awareness, and Cofense send realistic fake phishing emails to employees and measure click rates, report rates, and behavior change over time. The most mature programs create a culture where employees feel safe reporting mistakes without fear of punishment — because unreported incidents are far more damaging than reported ones.
NARRATION: Continuous, simulation-based security awareness training changes employee behavior more effectively than annual compliance modules ever can.

SLIDE 19 | Compliance Frameworks: GDPR, HIPAA, SOC 2, ISO 27001
Compliance frameworks provide structured requirements for protecting specific types of data and demonstrating security practices to customers, regulators, and partners. GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) governs personal data of EU residents, requiring lawful data collection, breach notification within 72 hours, and the right to erasure; violations carry fines up to 4% of global annual revenue. HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) protects personal health information (PHI) in the US healthcare sector, requiring administrative, physical, and technical safeguards. SOC 2 (System and Organization Controls 2) is an auditing standard for service organizations that demonstrates security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, and privacy controls to enterprise customers; achieving SOC 2 Type II certification (a 12-month audit period) is increasingly required to close enterprise B2B deals. ISO 27001 is an international standard for an Information Security Management System (ISMS), providing a systematic approach to managing sensitive information through risk assessment and formal controls.
NARRATION: Understanding GDPR, HIPAA, SOC 2, and ISO 27001 helps you build security programs that satisfy both regulatory requirements and enterprise customer expectations.

SLIDE 20 | CTF Competitions: Learning Security Through Practice
Capture the Flag (CTF) competitions are gamified cybersecurity challenges where participants solve security puzzles to find hidden "flags" — typically a string of text — earning points for each flag captured. CTFs are the most effective way for beginners to develop hands-on technical skills because they provide legal, sandboxed environments designed for exploitation practice without any real-world risk or legal exposure. Challenge categories mirror real security domains: web exploitation (OWASP vulnerabilities), binary exploitation (buffer overflows, reverse engineering), cryptography (breaking ciphers, RSA challenges), forensics (analyzing disk images and network captures), and steganography (hiding and finding data within files). Platforms like HackTheBox, TryHackMe, PicoCTF, and CTFtime.org aggregate competitions from around the world; TryHackMe in particular offers guided learning paths ideal for complete beginners who need structured progression before free-form challenges.
NARRATION: CTF competitions on platforms like HackTheBox and TryHackMe let you build real attack and defense skills in legal, sandboxed environments designed for learning.

SLIDE 21 | Security Certifications: Security+, CEH, CISSP, OSCP
Cybersecurity certifications validate your skills to employers and are often required for government, defense contractor, and enterprise security roles. CompTIA Security+ is the most widely recommended entry-level certification, covering threat management, cryptography, network security, and compliance — it satisfies DoD 8570 baseline requirements and is recognized across industries. Certified Ethical Hacker (CEH) from EC-Council covers offensive security methodology and is valued in penetration testing and red team roles, though practitioners debate whether the OSCP better demonstrates hands-on skill. The Offensive Security Certified Professional (OSCP) requires exploiting real machines in a 24-hour practical exam with no multiple choice — it is the gold standard for demonstrating hands-on penetration testing ability. CISSP (Certified Information Systems Security Professional) is aimed at experienced security managers and architects, requiring five years of experience, and is the most respected credential for security leadership roles.
NARRATION: Security+ provides the broadest entry-level foundation, while OSCP proves hands-on attack skills and CISSP validates security management expertise for senior roles.

SLIDE 22 | Blue Team, Red Team, and Purple Team
The security industry organizes specialized roles into color-coded teams that reflect their function and adversarial relationship. Blue teams defend — they operate the SOC (Security Operations Center), monitor SIEM alerts, respond to incidents, manage vulnerability patching, and harden systems. They work reactively against threats and proactively through threat hunting: searching through logs and telemetry for signs of attacker presence that automated alerts missed. Red teams attack — they conduct authorized penetration tests and adversary simulations using real attacker tools, tactics, and procedures (TTPs) to test whether blue team defenses would detect and respond to a real threat actor. Purple teams bridge the gap: red team attack findings are immediately shared with blue team defenders so that detections can be built and validated in real time, making the entire organization more resilient rather than leaving red team findings to sit in a report.
NARRATION: Blue teams defend and detect, red teams attack and test, and purple teams combine both to improve defenses in real time rather than waiting for a report.

SLIDE 23 | Cybersecurity Career Paths and Job Market
Cybersecurity is one of the fastest-growing career fields globally, with an estimated 3.5 million unfilled positions worldwide in 2025 — a chronic talent shortage that creates strong job security and above-average compensation for qualified practitioners. Entry-level roles include SOC Analyst (monitoring alerts, triaging incidents), IT Security Analyst (vulnerability management, policy compliance), and Security Engineer (building and maintaining security tooling). Mid-career paths branch into penetration tester / red team operator, threat intelligence analyst, cloud security engineer, security architect, and digital forensics investigator. Leadership paths lead to CISO (Chief Information Security Officer), security director, or GRC (Governance, Risk, and Compliance) manager roles. Non-technical professionals can contribute meaningfully in GRC, security awareness, policy writing, and vendor risk management without deep technical backgrounds.
NARRATION: The 3.5 million global shortage of cybersecurity professionals means qualified practitioners enter a job market with strong demand, competitive salaries, and many specialization paths.

SLIDE 24 | Building a Home Lab for Hands-On Practice
A home lab provides a safe environment for practicing offensive and defensive skills without any legal risk or impact on real systems. The simplest setup uses a consumer laptop running VirtualBox or VMware Workstation to host virtual machines: a Kali Linux VM for attacking and a vulnerable target VM like Metasploitable 2, VulnHub machines, or a Windows Server evaluation copy. Network segmentation in your hypervisor (host-only or NAT networks) keeps intentionally vulnerable machines isolated from your home network. Cloud platforms offer free-tier alternatives — AWS, Azure, and GCP all provide credits for students — and cloud labs can be torn down and rebuilt from scripts (using Terraform or CloudFormation) to practice infrastructure security at no permanent cost.
NARRATION: A home lab with virtual machines on a free hypervisor lets you safely practice real attack and defense techniques without risk to real systems or people.

SLIDE 25 | Threat Intelligence and Staying Current
Cyber threat intelligence (CTI) is the collection, analysis, and application of information about current and emerging threats — attacker TTPs, indicators of compromise (IOCs), and vulnerability disclosures — to improve an organization's defenses proactively. MITRE ATT&CK is a publicly available framework cataloging hundreds of real-world attacker techniques organized by tactic (Initial Access, Persistence, Lateral Movement, Exfiltration), used by red teams to structure assessments and blue teams to identify detection gaps. Staying current in cybersecurity requires consistent engagement with the community: following @SwiftOnSecurity and @troyhunt on Twitter/X, subscribing to Krebs on Security and The Hacker News, and checking CVE databases (NIST NVD, MITRE CVE) for newly disclosed vulnerabilities relevant to your environment. CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog lists vulnerabilities actively exploited in the wild and is the highest-priority patching list any defender can use.
NARRATION: MITRE ATT&CK and CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog give defenders a structured, evidence-based foundation for prioritizing detection and patching efforts.
