LESSON: Real Estate License Exam Prep
LANGUAGE: en

SLIDE 1 | Introduction to Real Estate Licensing
Real estate agents must be licensed by their state to represent buyers and sellers in
real estate transactions. The licensing exam has a national (uniform) portion and a
state-specific portion. Topics include property ownership, contracts, agency law, fair
housing, finance, and appraisal. Most states require pre-licensing education (typically
60-150 hours), a background check, and passage of the state exam.
NARRATION: The national portion of the exam tests principles that apply everywhere;
the state portion tests your specific state's license law and regulations.

SLIDE 2 | Types of Real Estate and Property Ownership
Real property includes land and anything permanently attached to it, such as buildings,
fences, and trees. Personal property (personalty or chattel) is movable property.
Items permanently affixed to real property are called fixtures; the test for a fixture
uses the acronym MARIA (Method of attachment, Adaptability, Relationship of the
parties, Intention, and Agreement). Real estate ownership types include fee simple
(most complete), life estate, and leasehold.
NARRATION: When a dispute arises about whether an item stays with the house, courts
use the MARIA test to determine if it is a fixture.

SLIDE 3 | Freehold vs Leasehold Estates
Freehold estates are ownership interests of indefinite duration. Fee simple absolute
is the most complete form of ownership, with no restrictions on use or transfer. A
fee simple defeasible can be lost if certain conditions occur. A life estate grants
ownership for the duration of a named person's life; the remainderman receives the
property after that person dies. Leasehold estates are non-ownership rental interests
for a defined period, creating a landlord-tenant relationship.
NARRATION: Fee simple absolute is what most homebuyers acquire; it is the broadest
form of real property ownership with the fewest restrictions.

SLIDE 4 | Legal Descriptions of Property
Legal descriptions precisely identify a parcel of land for legal documents. The metes
and bounds system describes boundaries by direction and distance, starting from a
point of beginning (POB) and returning to it. The rectangular (government) survey
system uses meridians, baselines, townships, and ranges to identify sections. The lot
and block system (recorded plat) is used in subdivisions and references a recorded
plat map. All three may appear on the exam.
NARRATION: Metes and bounds is the oldest system, used in the original thirteen
colonies; the rectangular survey system was created to describe the vast western
territories.

SLIDE 5 | Title and Types of Deeds
Title is the legal right to own and use property. A deed is the document that
transfers title from grantor (seller) to grantee (buyer). A general warranty deed
offers the most protection, with the grantor warranting title against all claims.
A special warranty deed warrants only against claims arising during the grantor's
ownership. A quitclaim deed conveys whatever interest the grantor has with no
warranties; used in divorce settlements and clearing clouds on title. A bargain and
sale deed conveys title with implied but not expressed warranty.
NARRATION: General warranty deeds provide the most buyer protection; quitclaim deeds
provide the least.

SLIDE 6 | Liens and Encumbrances
An encumbrance is any claim, lien, charge, or liability attached to real property.
Liens are financial encumbrances against a property. Specific liens attach to one
property (mortgage, mechanic's lien, property tax lien). General liens attach to all
property owned by the debtor (judgment lien, IRS lien). Liens are prioritized by
date recorded, except property tax liens which take priority over all others. A
mortgage must be paid off (satisfied) before clear title can be transferred.
NARRATION: Property tax liens always take first priority; even a first mortgage is
subordinate to an unpaid property tax lien.

SLIDE 7 | Agency Relationships
Agency is the legal relationship where one party (agent) acts on behalf of another
(principal). A seller's agent (listing agent) represents the seller. A buyer's agent
represents the buyer. Dual agency occurs when one agent represents both parties in
the same transaction; it requires written consent from both parties and is illegal
in some states. A transaction broker (non-agent) facilitates the transaction without
representing either party. Sub-agents work under the listing broker.
NARRATION: Dual agency creates a conflict of interest; the agent cannot fully advocate
for either party, which is why it requires informed consent.

SLIDE 8 | Fiduciary Duties - COALD
An agent owes fiduciary duties to the principal, summarized by the acronym COALD:
Confidentiality (do not share the client's personal information or motivation),
Obedience (follow lawful instructions), Accounting (account for all money and property),
Loyalty (put the client's interests first), and Disclosure (reveal all material facts).
These duties are owed to the principal only, not to the other party in the transaction.
NARRATION: Loyalty means the agent's interests and the other party's interests come
second; the client's interests always come first.

SLIDE 9 | Fair Housing Act and Protected Classes
The Federal Fair Housing Act (1968) prohibits discrimination in the sale, rental, and
financing of housing based on race, color, religion, national origin, sex, disability,
and familial status (seven federal classes). Many states add additional protected
classes such as sexual orientation, age, and source of income. Prohibited acts include
steering (directing buyers to or away from certain neighborhoods), blockbusting
(inducing panic selling), and redlining (refusing service in certain areas).
NARRATION: Steering, blockbusting, and redlining are all Fair Housing violations even
when the agent believes they are acting in the client's best interest.

SLIDE 10 | Listing Agreements
A listing agreement is a contract between a seller and a brokerage giving the broker
the right to market the property. An exclusive right to sell listing pays the
listing broker regardless of who finds the buyer; this is the most common type.
An exclusive agency listing pays the broker only if the broker finds the buyer;
the seller retains the right to sell without paying commission. An open listing is
non-exclusive and may be given to multiple brokers; commission goes to whoever
produces the buyer.
NARRATION: Exclusive right to sell listings best protect the broker's interests;
sellers prefer open listings but brokers rarely accept them.

SLIDE 11 | MLS and Property Marketing
The MLS (Multiple Listing Service) is a cooperative database shared among member
brokers that allows them to cooperate in selling listed properties. The listing broker
offers a commission split to buyer's brokers through the MLS. MLS input requires
accurate data; misrepresentation in MLS listings violates REALTOR ethics codes.
Marketing materials must include the brokerage name, comply with the National
Association of REALTORS (NAR) Code of Ethics, and follow state license law rules
on advertising.
NARRATION: MLS cooperation multiplies a property's exposure by sharing it with every
member broker's buyer pool; almost all residential listings are placed in the MLS.

SLIDE 12 | Contract Essentials
A valid real estate contract requires: mutual assent (offer and acceptance by legally
competent parties), consideration (something of value exchanged by both parties),
lawful purpose (legal objective), and must be in writing to be enforceable under
the Statute of Frauds. An offer becomes a contract upon acceptance and communication
of that acceptance back to the offeror. Counteroffers reject the original offer and
create a new offer. Either party may withdraw an offer before acceptance.
NARRATION: The Statute of Frauds requires real estate contracts to be in writing;
oral agreements to buy or sell real estate are generally not enforceable in court.

SLIDE 13 | Contingencies and Earnest Money
Contingencies are conditions that must be satisfied for the contract to be binding.
Common contingencies include financing (the buyer must obtain a mortgage), inspection
(the property must pass inspection), and appraisal (the property must appraise at or
above the purchase price). Earnest money is a deposit showing the buyer's good faith;
it is typically held in the listing broker's escrow account. If the buyer defaults,
the seller may retain the earnest money as liquidated damages.
NARRATION: Contingencies protect the buyer; if a contingency fails, the buyer can
back out and retrieve the earnest money.

SLIDE 14 | The Closing Process
Closing (settlement) is when title transfers from seller to buyer and funds are
disbursed. The Closing Disclosure (CD) replaced the HUD-1 Settlement Statement for
most transactions under TRID (TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure) rules. The CD must
be delivered three business days before closing. Closing costs include lender fees,
title insurance, prepaid interest, escrow setup, transfer taxes, and agent
commissions. Prorations (property tax, HOA dues, rent) are calculated as of the
closing date.
NARRATION: TRID requires the Closing Disclosure to be delivered three business days
before closing; last-minute changes may require a new three-day waiting period.

SLIDE 15 | Financing Basics - Mortgage Types
A conventional loan is not government-backed; it typically requires 20% down to
avoid PMI (private mortgage insurance). FHA loans are government-insured and allow
down payments as low as 3.5% but require mortgage insurance premiums. VA loans are
available to eligible veterans with no down payment. USDA loans serve rural buyers
with low to moderate incomes. Adjustable-rate mortgages (ARMs) have rates that
change after an initial fixed period; fixed-rate mortgages maintain the same rate
for the loan term.
NARRATION: FHA loans help first-time buyers with smaller down payments, but mortgage
insurance premiums add to the monthly cost throughout the loan term.

SLIDE 16 | Mortgage Underwriting
Underwriting evaluates a borrower's creditworthiness. Key factors are the Four Cs:
Credit (credit score and history), Capacity (debt-to-income ratio, typically 43% or
less), Capital (assets and reserves), and Collateral (property value via appraisal).
A loan-to-value ratio (LTV) above 80% typically requires PMI. Lenders verify income
through W-2s, pay stubs, and tax returns. Self-employed borrowers must provide two
years of tax returns.
NARRATION: The debt-to-income ratio compares monthly debt payments to gross monthly
income; keeping it below 43% is the standard underwriting benchmark.

SLIDE 17 | Property Valuation - Comparative Market Analysis
A Comparative Market Analysis (CMA) estimates a property's market value by comparing
it to recently sold similar properties (comps) in the same area. Agents perform CMAs
to help sellers price their homes and buyers make competitive offers. Adjustments are
made for differences in size, condition, location, and features. A CMA is not the same
as a formal appraisal; only licensed appraisers may produce legally recognized
appraisals for lenders.
NARRATION: A CMA gives sellers realistic pricing guidance; overpricing leads to
longer days on market and eventual price reductions.

SLIDE 18 | The Three Approaches to Value
Licensed appraisers use three approaches to estimate market value. The Sales Comparison
Approach compares the subject property to recent sales of comparable properties and is
most reliable for residential properties. The Income Approach values property based on
its income-producing potential (Net Operating Income divided by the capitalization rate)
and is used for rental and commercial properties. The Cost Approach estimates the cost
to rebuild the improvements plus land value, less depreciation; used for special-use
properties.
NARRATION: Appraisers give the most weight to the approach that best fits the property
type; the sales comparison approach is almost always primary for single-family homes.

SLIDE 19 | License Law and Ethics
Each state's real estate license law governs who must be licensed, license requirements,
renewal, and grounds for discipline. Common violations include commingling (mixing
client funds with personal funds), conversion (using client funds for personal use),
misrepresentation, and practicing without a license. The NAR Code of Ethics requires
REALTORS to treat all parties honestly, cooperate with other brokers, and put client
interests first. Ethics violations are handled by local REALTOR associations.
NARRATION: Commingling and conversion are among the most serious license violations;
always keep client trust funds in a separate escrow account.

SLIDE 20 | Property Management Basics
Property managers oversee rental properties on behalf of owners. Responsibilities
include setting rents, marketing vacancies, screening tenants, executing leases,
collecting rent, maintaining the property, and handling evictions. The Americans with
Disabilities Act (ADA) and Fair Housing Act impose requirements for reasonable
accommodations and modifications for disabled tenants. Security deposits are regulated
by state law; they must be held in separate accounts and returned within a specified
period after move-out.
NARRATION: Security deposit laws vary by state; failure to return deposits within the
legal deadline can result in penalties of two to three times the deposit amount.

SLIDE 21 | Environmental Issues in Real Estate
Agents must disclose known material defects including environmental hazards. Lead-
based paint disclosure is required for homes built before 1978. Asbestos is hazardous
when disturbed; its presence must be disclosed. Radon is a radioactive gas that seeps
from soil; testing and mitigation are available. Underground storage tanks (USTs) can
contaminate soil. Wetlands are federally regulated; building in wetlands requires
permits. Agents are not inspectors but must disclose known issues.
NARRATION: Lead paint disclosure is a federal requirement; failure to provide the
required disclosure form before contract can result in a $10,000 fine.

SLIDE 22 | Land Use Controls
Zoning ordinances are local laws that regulate land use by dividing areas into zones
(residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural). Zoning changes require action
by local government. A variance permits a deviation from zoning requirements. A
nonconforming use is a pre-existing use that no longer conforms to current zoning.
Eminent domain allows government to take private property for public use with just
compensation. Deed restrictions (CC&Rs) are private land use controls enforced
between property owners.
NARRATION: Eminent domain requires just compensation to the property owner; it is
not confiscation but a compelled purchase at market value.

SLIDE 23 | Investment Real Estate Concepts
Cap rate (capitalization rate) = Net Operating Income (NOI) / Property Value. A higher
cap rate indicates higher risk or lower property value. Cash-on-cash return measures
annual pre-tax cash flow as a percentage of the cash invested. Leverage uses borrowed
money to increase return on equity. Depreciation (cost recovery) allows investors to
deduct a portion of the property's cost each year for income tax purposes. A 1031
exchange allows investors to defer capital gains taxes by reinvesting in like-kind
property.
NARRATION: The 1031 exchange is one of real estate investing's most powerful tax
deferral tools; it allows investors to swap one property for another while deferring
capital gains tax.

SLIDE 24 | Real Estate Math Essentials
Key formulas: Commission = Sales Price x Commission Rate. Profit/Loss = Selling Price
minus (Purchase Price + Costs). Prorated amounts = (Annual amount / 365) x days.
LTV = Loan Amount / Property Value. Mortgage payment tables or financial calculators
are used for amortization. Area of a rectangle = length x width (in square feet).
Converting square feet to acres: divide by 43,560. Practice all formulas because math
questions appear on every state exam.
NARRATION: One acre equals 43,560 square feet; memorize this conversion because it
appears on virtually every real estate exam.

SLIDE 25 | Exam Format and Preparation Strategy
Most state real estate exams have 80-150 questions divided between national and state
content, with a time limit of two to four hours. Passing scores are typically 70-75%.
Exams are administered by PSI or Pearson VUE at testing centers. Use your pre-licensing
course materials plus state-approved exam prep tools. Take at least 300-400 practice
questions before your exam day. Focus on vocabulary, math formulas, and the nuances
between similar-sounding concepts (e.g., general vs special warranty deed).
NARRATION: Real estate exam questions often test your ability to distinguish between
very similar concepts; reading carefully and knowing precise definitions is critical.

SLIDE 26 | After the License - Getting Started
After passing the exam, new licensees must affiliate with a licensed brokerage to
activate their license. New agents typically start as buyer's agents. Mentorship
programs and shadowing experienced agents accelerate learning. Build a sphere of
influence (SOI) through networking, social media, and community involvement. Understand
that most commission income arrives after 60-90 days in the pipeline; budget for this
delay. Continuing education is required to renew your license every two to four years
depending on the state.
NARRATION: Your first six months will be slow as you build your pipeline; consistency
in prospecting and follow-up determines whether you succeed in real estate.
