SLIDE 1 | What is ASE Certification?
ASE (Automotive Service Excellence) certifications are the industry standard for
proving automotive technician competency. Founded in 1972, ASE tests and certifies
professionals in automobile, truck, collision repair, and service consultant roles.
Most employers require ASE certification; many customers specifically request ASE-
certified technicians. Certification requires passing a written test and demonstrating
two years of hands-on work experience.
NARRATION: ASE certification is proof that you know your trade; employers and
customers trust the ASE seal as a mark of quality.

SLIDE 2 | ASE Test Series Overview
The automobile technician series includes A1 (Engine Repair), A2 (Automatic
Transmission/Transaxle), A3 (Manual Drive Train and Axles), A4 (Suspension and
Steering), A5 (Brakes), A6 (Electrical/Electronic Systems), A7 (Heating and Air
Conditioning), A8 (Engine Performance), and A9 (Light Vehicle Diesel Engines).
Advanced certifications include L1 (Advanced Engine Performance Specialist), L2
(Electronic Diesel Engine Diagnosis), L3 (Light Duty Hybrid/Electric Vehicle), and
G1 (Auto Maintenance and Light Repair).
NARRATION: Earning all A-series certifications plus L1 makes you an ASE Master
Automobile Technician, the highest designation in the field.

SLIDE 3 | Engine Repair Fundamentals - Engine Construction
Internal combustion engines convert fuel energy into mechanical work. Key components
include the cylinder block (engine's main structure), pistons (reciprocate to compress
and transfer force), connecting rods (link pistons to the crankshaft), the crankshaft
(converts reciprocating motion to rotation), and the cylinder head (contains valves,
combustion chamber, and in many engines the camshaft). The head gasket seals between
block and head.
NARRATION: A blown head gasket allows coolant and combustion gases to mix, causing
overheating and engine damage; it is one of the most common A1 diagnosis topics.

SLIDE 4 | Valvetrain and Timing
The valvetrain controls the opening and closing of intake and exhaust valves. It
includes camshafts, lifters (tappets), pushrods (on OHV engines), rocker arms, and
valves. OHC (overhead cam) engines place the camshaft in the cylinder head. DOHC
engines have two camshafts per head, one for intake and one for exhaust. The timing
belt or chain synchronizes the crankshaft and camshaft. A broken timing belt can
cause catastrophic engine damage on interference engines.
NARRATION: Timing belt replacement at the manufacturer's interval prevents interference
engine destruction; missing this service is an expensive mistake.

SLIDE 5 | Engine Seals, Gaskets, and Lubrication
Engine seals prevent oil and coolant leaks. Common seals include crankshaft front and
rear main seals, valve stem seals (control oil consumption), and intake manifold
gaskets. The oil pump draws oil from the sump and circulates it under pressure to
bearings, the valvetrain, and other moving parts. Oil viscosity (e.g., 5W-30) indicates
cold and hot flow characteristics. Regular oil changes prevent sludge and bearing wear.
NARRATION: Low oil pressure is a critical warning; driving with low oil pressure
will destroy bearings within minutes.

SLIDE 6 | Engine Performance - Fuel Injection Systems
Modern vehicles use electronic fuel injection (EFI). The ECM (Engine Control Module)
calculates fuel delivery based on inputs from sensors including the Mass Airflow (MAF)
sensor, Manifold Absolute Pressure (MAP) sensor, Throttle Position Sensor (TPS),
Oxygen sensors (O2), and Engine Coolant Temperature (ECT) sensor. Port injection
sprays fuel at the intake valve; direct injection sprays fuel directly into the
combustion chamber for better efficiency.
NARRATION: The ECM is the brain of fuel injection; faulty sensors send bad data to
the ECM, causing rich or lean conditions.

SLIDE 7 | Ignition Systems
Modern ignition systems use coil-on-plug (COP) or coil-near-plug designs with no
distributor. The PCM controls ignition timing electronically. Spark plugs ignite the
air-fuel mixture; they should be replaced per manufacturer intervals. A misfiring
cylinder sets a P030X DTC (X = cylinder number). Ignition timing advance improves
power and efficiency but too much advance causes detonation (knock), which can
damage pistons.
NARRATION: A misfire code like P0301 means cylinder 1 is misfiring; check the spark
plug, ignition coil, and fuel injector for that cylinder.

SLIDE 8 | Emissions Systems
Exhaust emissions are controlled by the catalytic converter, EGR (Exhaust Gas
Recirculation) valve, EVAP (Evaporative Emission Control) system, PCV (Positive
Crankcase Ventilation) valve, and oxygen sensors. The catalytic converter converts
CO, HC, and NOx into CO2, H2O, and N2. A failing cat causes P0420/P0430 codes.
The EVAP system captures fuel vapors from the tank; a loose gas cap is the most
common cause of EVAP codes.
NARRATION: P0420 (catalyst efficiency below threshold) is one of the most common
check engine codes; it usually means the catalytic converter needs replacement.

SLIDE 9 | Automatic Transmissions and Transaxles
An automatic transmission uses a torque converter instead of a clutch to transfer
engine power, and planetary gear sets controlled by hydraulic and electronic solenoids
to select gear ratios. The TCM (Transmission Control Module) controls shift timing
based on throttle position, speed, and load. Common issues include slipping,
shuddering, and harsh shifts. Automatic transmission fluid (ATF) type must match
manufacturer specification exactly.
NARRATION: Using the wrong ATF type can damage clutch packs and shift solenoids;
always verify the correct fluid before servicing.

SLIDE 10 | Manual Drivetrain and Axles
Manual transmissions use a friction clutch operated by the driver to engage and
disengage engine power during gear changes. Synchronizers allow smooth gear engagement
without grinding. Clutch components include the pressure plate, clutch disc, throwout
bearing, and flywheel. CV (constant velocity) joints on front-wheel-drive vehicles
allow the axle to flex while maintaining constant speed; a clicking noise in turns
indicates a failing outer CV joint.
NARRATION: A clicking sound during tight turns is the classic sign of a worn outer
CV joint; replace the CV axle before complete failure.

SLIDE 11 | Suspension and Steering Systems
The suspension system absorbs road impacts and keeps tires in contact with the road.
Components include springs (coil, leaf, torsion bar), shock absorbers, struts, control
arms, and ball joints. Worn ball joints or tie rod ends cause steering looseness and
create unsafe handling. Wheel alignment angles (camber, caster, toe) affect tire wear
and handling. Power steering uses hydraulic or electric assistance to reduce steering
effort.
NARRATION: Uneven tire wear is often a symptom of alignment or suspension issues;
always inspect suspension components before performing an alignment.

SLIDE 12 | Brake Systems - Disc Brakes
Disc brakes use a caliper to squeeze brake pads against a rotor to slow the vehicle.
Floating calipers use one piston; fixed calipers use multiple pistons for better
performance. Brake pad friction material transfers to the rotor surface during break-
in. Minimum rotor thickness is stamped on the rotor; rotors thinner than spec must
be replaced. Brake dust and heat are hazards during disc brake service.
NARRATION: Always measure rotor thickness after resurfacing; a rotor at minimum
thickness must be replaced, not turned.

SLIDE 13 | Brake Systems - Drum Brakes and ABS
Drum brakes use wheel cylinders to push brake shoes against the inside of a rotating
drum. They are typically used on the rear wheels of economy vehicles. Self-adjusters
maintain proper shoe-to-drum clearance. ABS (Anti-lock Braking System) uses wheel
speed sensors to detect lockup and rapidly modulates brake pressure to maintain
steering control during hard stops. ABS codes are stored separately from powertrain
codes.
NARRATION: ABS allows you to steer while braking hard; without ABS, a locked wheel
means you lose the ability to steer away from an obstacle.

SLIDE 14 | Brake System Hydraulics and Safety
The master cylinder converts pedal force to hydraulic pressure. Brake lines carry
fluid to calipers and wheel cylinders. A spongy pedal indicates air in the system;
a low pedal that firms up with pumping suggests a failing master cylinder. Brake fluid
is hygroscopic and absorbs moisture over time, lowering its boiling point. DOT 3, 4,
and 5.1 are glycol-based; DOT 5 is silicone-based and must not be mixed with glycol
fluids.
NARRATION: Never mix DOT 5 silicone fluid with DOT 3 or 4 glycol fluid; mixing causes
the fluid to gel and brakes to fail catastrophically.

SLIDE 15 | Electrical Systems - Battery and Charging
The battery provides starting current and stabilizes electrical system voltage. Battery
capacity is measured in CCA (Cold Cranking Amps) and reserve capacity. The alternator
recharges the battery during operation, typically outputting 13.5-14.5 volts. A
charging system below 13 volts indicates a failing alternator or poor connections. Battery
load testing reveals internal weakness that a simple voltage test can miss.
NARRATION: A battery may read 12.6 volts at rest but fail under load; always load-test
a battery before condemning the charging system.

SLIDE 16 | Electrical Systems - Starting and Lighting
The starting system includes the battery, ignition switch, neutral safety switch (or
clutch switch), starter relay or solenoid, and starter motor. A click with no crank
indicates a bad solenoid or dead battery; no click at all points to the relay, switch,
or safety switch. Vehicle lighting uses a combination of incandescent, halogen, HID
(xenon), and LED bulbs. LED headlights draw less current and last longer but require
proper aiming.
NARRATION: Diagnose starting problems systematically: verify battery voltage, then
test the relay, then the solenoid, then the starter motor itself.

SLIDE 17 | Heating and Air Conditioning (A7)
Vehicle HVAC systems use the same refrigeration cycle as home AC. The compressor is
belt-driven and controlled by a magnetic clutch. R-134a is the refrigerant in most
pre-2021 vehicles; newer vehicles may use R-1234yf. Technicians must be EPA 609
certified to purchase and handle automotive refrigerants. Heater cores use engine
coolant to heat the passenger compartment. Blend doors control temperature mixing.
NARRATION: EPA 609 is the automotive equivalent of Section 608; it is required to
purchase refrigerant for vehicle AC service.

SLIDE 18 | Engine Maintenance Services
Routine engine maintenance includes oil and filter changes (every 5,000-10,000 miles
for synthetic oil), air filter inspection, fuel filter replacement, spark plug replacement,
coolant system flush, timing belt replacement (if equipped), serpentine belt inspection,
and PCV valve replacement. Following manufacturer maintenance schedules prevents
breakdowns. Neglecting maintenance is the leading cause of premature engine failure.
NARRATION: Regular oil changes are the single most important maintenance service;
clean oil protects bearing surfaces from metal-to-metal contact.

SLIDE 19 | OBD-II Diagnostics and Scan Tools
OBD-II (On-Board Diagnostics II) became mandatory on all U.S. vehicles starting in
1996. The system monitors hundreds of parameters and stores Diagnostic Trouble Codes
(DTCs) when a fault is detected. The MIL (Malfunction Indicator Lamp, or check engine
light) illuminates when an emissions-related fault is stored. Scan tools connect to
the OBD-II port (DLC) under the dashboard to read codes, view live data, and perform
active tests.
NARRATION: The OBD-II port is always within 18 inches of the steering wheel; it is your
primary diagnostic gateway to the vehicle's computer systems.

SLIDE 20 | Reading and Diagnosing DTCs
DTCs have a five-character format: a letter (P for powertrain, B for body, C for
chassis, U for network) followed by four digits. The second character (0 or 1)
indicates generic or manufacturer-specific. Common codes include P0300 (random
misfire), P0171 (system lean bank 1), P0420 (catalyst efficiency), and P0455 (EVAP
large leak). Always research the code and follow a diagnostic procedure rather than
replacing parts blindly.
NARRATION: A DTC identifies the system with the fault, not necessarily the failed
part; diagnose the root cause before replacing components.

SLIDE 21 | Customer Service in Automotive
Building customer trust is critical in automotive service. Always explain repairs in
plain language, provide written estimates before beginning work, and obtain customer
authorization. Inform customers of any additional findings discovered during service.
Avoid high-pressure upselling; recommend maintenance items honestly. Keep the service
area clean, return vehicles cleaner than received, and follow up after repairs to
ensure satisfaction.
NARRATION: Repeat customers and referrals are the lifeblood of a shop; excellent
communication builds the trust that keeps customers coming back.

SLIDE 22 | Shop Safety
OSHA regulations govern workplace safety in automotive shops. Use personal protective
equipment (PPE) including safety glasses, gloves, and hearing protection. Lift vehicles
only on designated lift points shown in the service manual. Never work under a vehicle
supported only by a floor jack; always use jack stands. Keep the shop floor clean and
dry to prevent slips. Dispose of used oil, coolant, and brake fluid at approved
recycling facilities.
NARRATION: A shop injury costs you time and money; PPE, proper lifting techniques,
and a clean workspace prevent the majority of shop accidents.

SLIDE 23 | ASE Exam Format and Preparation
ASE exams are administered by Prometric at authorized testing centers. Each A-series
exam has 40-80 multiple-choice questions. The passing score is approximately 70%.
Questions are based on the ASE task list for each test. Study using official ASE study
guides, Delmar's ASE test prep books, and free practice tests at the ASE website.
The exam covers theory, diagnosis, and service procedures. Hands-on experience is
the best preparation.
NARRATION: The ASE exam tests both knowledge and the ability to apply it; two years
of hands-on experience before testing dramatically improves pass rates.

SLIDE 24 | Hybrid and Electric Vehicle Basics (L3)
Hybrid vehicles combine an internal combustion engine with one or more electric motors
and a high-voltage battery pack. High-voltage systems in hybrids typically operate
at 144-650 volts and require special precautions. Always wear Class 0 high-voltage
gloves when working near orange high-voltage cables. The L3 certification covers
diagnosis and repair of hybrid and EV systems including battery management, electric
motor service, and regenerative braking.
NARRATION: High-voltage systems can kill in milliseconds; never assume a hybrid or EV
is safe to touch without proper PPE and training.

SLIDE 25 | Fuel Systems and Fuel Trim
The fuel system delivers pressurized fuel from the tank to the injectors. Components
include the fuel pump, filter, fuel pressure regulator, and injectors. Fuel trim is
the ECM's long-term correction to the fuel delivery calculation. Positive fuel trim
(LTFT +) means the engine is running lean; the ECM is adding fuel. Negative fuel trim
means the engine runs rich; the ECM is reducing fuel. Fuel trim outside plus or minus
10% indicates a problem.
NARRATION: Fuel trim is a window into engine health; high positive trim often points
to a vacuum leak or failing fuel pump.

SLIDE 26 | ASE Recertification
ASE certifications are valid for five years. To recertify, you must pass the current
version of each exam before your certification expires. ASE sends renewal notices in
advance. Staying current with technology is important because vehicle systems change
rapidly. Consider advancing to L1, L2, or L3 specialist certifications after earning
the A-series to increase your value and earning potential.
NARRATION: Recertification every five years keeps your skills current with evolving
technology; expired ASE certificates cannot be displayed or claimed.
