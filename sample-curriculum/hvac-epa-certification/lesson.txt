LESSON: HVAC and EPA 608 Certification Prep
LANGUAGE: en

SLIDE 1 | What is EPA Section 608?
EPA Section 608 of the Clean Air Act regulates the handling of refrigerants used in
stationary refrigeration and air conditioning equipment. Technicians who purchase or
handle refrigerants must be certified. Violations can result in fines up to $44,539
per day per violation. Certification is administered through EPA-approved testing
organizations.
NARRATION: Section 608 protects the ozone layer by requiring certified technicians to
handle refrigerants responsibly and avoid illegal venting.

SLIDE 2 | Refrigerant Categories - CFCs, HCFCs, and HFCs
CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) such as R-11 and R-12 have the highest ozone depletion
potential (ODP) and are fully phased out. HCFCs (hydrochlorofluorocarbons) such as
R-22 have lower ODP and are being phased out under the Montreal Protocol. HFCs
(hydrofluorocarbons) such as R-410A and R-134a have zero ODP but high global warming
potential (GWP). HFOs are the newest low-GWP alternatives.
NARRATION: Know the three main refrigerant families by their ozone impact; CFCs are
banned, HCFCs are being phased out, and HFCs are current but face future restrictions.

SLIDE 3 | Common Refrigerants - R-22
R-22 (chlorodifluoromethane) is an HCFC once used in most residential air conditioners.
It has an ODP of 0.05 and a GWP of 1,810. The U.S. banned new production and import
of R-22 as of January 1, 2020. Only reclaimed or recovered R-22 may be used for
servicing existing equipment. Technicians must use Type II or Universal certification
to handle it.
NARRATION: R-22 is still found in older systems; you may service them with reclaimed
refrigerant, but you cannot buy virgin R-22 anymore.

SLIDE 4 | Common Refrigerants - R-410A and R-32
R-410A is an HFC blend (50% R-32 and 50% R-125) used as the primary replacement for
R-22 in residential systems. It operates at higher pressures than R-22 and requires
dedicated tools and recovery equipment. R-32 is a single-component HFC with lower
GWP (675) than R-410A (2,088) and is becoming more common in newer systems. Neither
depletes ozone.
NARRATION: R-410A dominates current residential installs, but R-32's lower GWP makes
it the direction the industry is heading.

SLIDE 5 | Certification Types - Type I
Type I certification covers small appliances. These are factory-sealed systems
with five pounds or less of refrigerant, such as household refrigerators, freezers,
window air conditioners, and packaged terminal units. Recovery requirements differ
for Type I: technicians may use system-dependent (passive) recovery equipment. The
refrigerant is typically R-134a or R-600a (isobutane).
NARRATION: Type I is the small appliance category; passive recovery is allowed because
the systems are factory-sealed and small.

SLIDE 6 | Certification Types - Type II
Type II certification covers high-pressure appliances, which are systems that use
refrigerants with a boiling point above -50 degrees C at atmospheric pressure, such
as R-22, R-410A, and R-134a. Most residential and commercial air conditioning and
refrigeration systems fall into this category. Type II requires active (self-contained)
recovery equipment and more stringent leak repair requirements.
NARRATION: Type II is the most common certification for residential and light commercial
HVAC work; most technicians start here.

SLIDE 7 | Certification Types - Type III and Universal
Type III certification covers low-pressure appliances using refrigerants with a boiling
point below -50 degrees C at atmospheric pressure, such as R-11 and R-113 used in
large commercial chillers. Pressures during recovery can drop below atmospheric,
creating vacuum conditions. Universal certification covers all three types and is the
most versatile credential for commercial HVAC technicians.
NARRATION: Universal certification is the gold standard; it allows you to work on any
refrigeration or air conditioning system.

SLIDE 8 | The Refrigeration Cycle - Overview
The basic refrigeration cycle has four stages: compression, condensation, expansion,
and evaporation. The refrigerant changes state between liquid and vapor to absorb
and release heat. This cycle moves heat from inside a space (where it is unwanted) to
outside. The key principle is that refrigerants absorb heat when they evaporate and
release heat when they condense.
NARRATION: Refrigeration does not create cold; it moves heat from one place to another
using the refrigerant's phase-change properties.

SLIDE 9 | Refrigeration Cycle - Compression
The compressor receives low-pressure refrigerant vapor from the evaporator and
compresses it into a high-pressure, high-temperature vapor. This is the heart of the
system. Common compressor types include reciprocating, scroll, rotary, and centrifugal.
The compressor is driven by an electric motor. High compressor head pressure may
indicate a dirty condenser or refrigerant overcharge.
NARRATION: The compressor is the workhorse of the refrigeration system; it raises both
pressure and temperature of the refrigerant vapor.

SLIDE 10 | Refrigeration Cycle - Condensation and Expansion
The high-pressure vapor flows to the condenser, where it releases heat to the outside
air or water and condenses into a high-pressure liquid. The liquid then passes through
the expansion device (TXV or orifice) which lowers its pressure rapidly. This pressure
drop causes the refrigerant to flash, dropping its temperature dramatically before it
enters the evaporator.
NARRATION: The condenser rejects heat; the expansion valve is the pressure dividing
point between the high and low sides of the system.

SLIDE 11 | Refrigeration Cycle - Evaporation
The low-pressure liquid refrigerant enters the evaporator coil and absorbs heat from
the surrounding air or product, causing it to evaporate back into a vapor. This is
the cooling effect you feel from an air conditioner or refrigerator. The low-pressure
vapor then returns to the compressor and the cycle repeats. Superheat and subcooling
measurements verify proper refrigerant charge.
NARRATION: The evaporator produces the cooling effect; superheat ensures all liquid
has evaporated before returning to the compressor to prevent damage.

SLIDE 12 | Refrigerant Recovery Procedures
Federal law prohibits venting refrigerants into the atmosphere. Recovery must occur
before opening or disposing of any system. Self-contained (active) recovery machines
are required for most equipment. Recovery continues until the system reaches the
required vacuum level specified by EPA regulations. Recovered refrigerant must be
stored in approved DOT cylinders and labeled with the refrigerant type.
NARRATION: Always recover before opening any system; venting refrigerant is a federal
violation with serious financial penalties.

SLIDE 13 | Recycling and Reclaiming Refrigerants
Recycling means cleaning refrigerant for reuse in the same or similar equipment using
oil separation and moisture removal, but without meeting ARI 700 purity standards.
Reclaiming means processing refrigerant to ARI 700 purity standards at an EPA-certified
facility, making it equivalent to new refrigerant. Reclaimed refrigerant can be sold
and used in any compatible equipment. Only EPA-certified reclaimers may reclaim.
NARRATION: Recycled refrigerant stays on-site; reclaimed refrigerant is purified to
new-product standards and can be resold.

SLIDE 14 | Leak Detection Requirements
EPA requires technicians to repair leaks when the leak rate exceeds the threshold for
the equipment type. For commercial refrigeration, the threshold is 20% of the charge
per year; for comfort cooling chillers it is 10%. Methods of leak detection include
electronic detectors, UV dye, soap bubbles, and nitrogen pressure testing. After repair,
technicians must verify the repair and document it.
NARRATION: Leak detection is not optional; you must repair leaks above the threshold
and verify the repair was successful.

SLIDE 15 | Venting Prohibitions and Safe Refrigerants
It is illegal to knowingly vent any CFC, HCFC, or HFC refrigerant while maintaining,
servicing, repairing, or disposing of equipment. Small releases incidental to good-
faith recovery are allowed. Certain substitute refrigerants designated as acceptable
by EPA under the SNAP program may be vented under specific conditions. Nitrogen used
for pressure testing is not regulated and may be vented.
NARRATION: The rule is simple: never intentionally vent regulated refrigerants;
nitrogen is not a refrigerant and is not subject to venting rules.

SLIDE 16 | Refrigerant Cylinders and Safety
Refrigerant is stored in color-coded cylinders by type (e.g., pink for R-22, rose/
light green for R-410A). Recovery cylinders are gray with yellow tops. Never fill a
cylinder beyond 80% capacity; refrigerants expand with heat. Store cylinders upright,
secured, and away from heat sources. Never heat cylinders with open flames. Cylinders
must be DOT-approved and hydrostatically tested.
NARRATION: Color coding and the 80% fill rule are safety standards that prevent
cylinder rupture from thermal expansion.

SLIDE 17 | Record-Keeping Requirements
Technicians must keep records of refrigerant purchases and transfers. Appliances
with more than 50 pounds of refrigerant require records when refrigerant is added,
including the date, amount added, and technician's name. Records must be kept for
three years and made available to the EPA upon request. Distributors must verify
purchaser certification before selling refrigerants in containers larger than two
pounds.
NARRATION: Documentation protects technicians; if the EPA audits you, your records
prove compliance with recovery and leak repair requirements.

SLIDE 18 | Electrical Safety for HVAC Technicians
Always lock out and tag out (LOTO) electrical panels before servicing equipment.
Use a multimeter to verify circuits are de-energized before touching wires.
Capacitors in HVAC equipment store lethal charges even after power is removed;
discharge them with a resistor before handling. Understand single-phase and three-
phase power, common voltages (115V, 208V, 230V, 460V), and ampacity requirements
for equipment.
NARRATION: Capacitors are the biggest electrical hazard in HVAC; they can hold
enough charge to kill even after the unit is unplugged.

SLIDE 19 | Troubleshooting Refrigeration Systems
Systematic troubleshooting starts with collecting symptom information from the
customer. Check pressures on both the high and low sides using a manifold gauge set.
Compare suction and discharge pressures to normal operating ranges for the refrigerant
type. Measure superheat and subcooling. Common problems include low charge (low
suction pressure), overcharge (high suction pressure), and dirty condenser (high
discharge pressure).
NARRATION: Use your manifold gauges as your diagnostic tool; suction pressure reflects
evaporator conditions and discharge pressure reflects condenser conditions.

SLIDE 20 | EPA Penalties for Violations
Knowingly venting a regulated refrigerant can result in civil penalties up to $44,539
per day per violation. Purchasing refrigerant without proper certification is also
a violation. EPA may pay rewards of up to $10,000 to individuals who provide
information leading to a penalty. Criminal penalties may apply for willful violations.
Companies may also lose the ability to purchase refrigerants.
NARRATION: The financial penalties for violations far exceed any money saved by
skipping proper recovery; certification protects your livelihood.

SLIDE 21 | Refrigerant Oils and Compatibility
Different refrigerants require compatible lubricating oils. R-22 systems use mineral
oil or alkylbenzene oil. R-410A and most HFC systems require polyolester (POE) oil,
which is hygroscopic and must be kept sealed to prevent moisture absorption. Mixing
incompatible oils or refrigerants can damage compressors and void warranties. Always
flush systems when converting from one refrigerant type to another.
NARRATION: POE oil absorbs moisture rapidly from air; keep containers sealed and
work quickly to prevent contamination.

SLIDE 22 | Pressure-Enthalpy and System Efficiency
A pressure-enthalpy (P-H) diagram maps the refrigeration cycle and helps diagnose
efficiency problems. SEER (Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio) rates cooling efficiency
for air conditioners; higher SEER is more efficient. EER measures efficiency at a
specific operating point. HSPF rates heat pump heating efficiency. Modern equipment
must meet minimum SEER2 ratings set by the Department of Energy.
NARRATION: SEER ratings help customers understand energy costs; a higher SEER unit
costs more upfront but saves money on utility bills over time.

SLIDE 23 | EPA 608 Exam Format
The EPA 608 exam has a core section taken by all candidates plus a section for each
certification type sought. The core section covers environmental impact, regulations,
and safety. Type I, II, and III sections cover specific equipment and procedures.
A score of 70% or higher is required to pass. Exams are administered by EPA-approved
testing organizations. Certification does not expire; it is a one-time credential.
NARRATION: Plan to take the core plus the type sections you need; Universal candidates
take all four sections in one sitting.

SLIDE 24 | Montreal Protocol and Global Phase-Down
The Montreal Protocol is an international treaty signed in 1987 to phase out ozone-
depleting substances. It successfully eliminated CFC production globally and set
phase-down schedules for HCFCs. The Kigali Amendment (2016) added HFC phase-down
targets due to their high global warming potential. The U.S. is implementing HFC
reductions through the AIM Act, which will drive adoption of lower-GWP refrigerants
like HFOs.
NARRATION: The Montreal Protocol is one of the most successful environmental treaties
in history; HVAC technicians play a direct role in its success through proper
refrigerant handling.

SLIDE 25 | HFO Refrigerants and the Future
Hydrofluoroolefins (HFOs) such as R-1234yf and R-1234ze have near-zero GWP and
zero ODP, making them the environmentally preferred refrigerants. R-1234yf is
replacing R-134a in automotive air conditioning. HFO blends like R-454B are being
used in new residential equipment to replace R-410A. HFOs are mildly flammable
(A2L classification), requiring updated safety procedures and equipment.
NARRATION: The industry is moving toward HFOs; technicians must understand A2L
flammability requirements and updated handling procedures for these new refrigerants.

SLIDE 26 | Study Tips and Certification Resources
Study using EPA 608 prep books and free online practice tests available through
HVAC-R industry associations. Focus on the differences between refrigerant categories,
recovery requirements for each type, and the specific thresholds and percentages
tested on the exam. The Refrigeration Service Engineers Society (RSES) and ESCO Group
offer study materials and approved testing. Practice calculating recovery efficiency
and leak rate percentages.
NARRATION: The 608 exam rewards rote knowledge of regulations and numbers; create
flashcards for thresholds, penalties, and refrigerant properties.
