LESSON: CPR and First Aid Certification
LANGUAGE: en

SLIDE 1 | Why CPR and First Aid Matter
Cardiac arrest kills more than 350,000 Americans outside of hospitals each year. When
CPR is started within two minutes of cardiac arrest, survival rates can double or
triple. Basic first aid skills allow bystanders to stabilize injuries, stop bleeding,
and support breathing until emergency medical services arrive. CPR and first aid
certification is required for many jobs in healthcare, education, childcare, and fitness.
NARRATION: Every minute without CPR after cardiac arrest reduces the chance of survival
by seven to ten percent; bystander action is the most important factor in survival.

SLIDE 2 | Recognizing a Cardiac Emergency
Cardiac arrest occurs when the heart suddenly stops beating effectively. Signs include
sudden collapse, unresponsiveness, no normal breathing (may see gasping or agonal
breathing), and no pulse. A heart attack (myocardial infarction) is caused by a
blocked artery and may or may not lead to cardiac arrest. Heart attack symptoms include
chest pain or pressure, pain radiating to the arm or jaw, shortness of breath,
nausea, and cold sweats. Call 911 immediately for either.
NARRATION: Agonal gasping is not normal breathing; if someone collapses and is not
responding normally, assume cardiac arrest and start CPR.

SLIDE 3 | Calling 911 and the Chain of Survival
The American Heart Association's Chain of Survival has six links: recognize the
emergency and call 911, perform CPR immediately, use an AED as soon as available,
transfer to advanced emergency care, hospital care, and recovery. Every link matters.
When calling 911, stay on the line, provide your location first, describe what happened,
and follow dispatcher instructions. In large groups, designate a specific person to call
911 and another to get the AED.
NARRATION: Calling 911 is always the first step; early CPR and early defibrillation
are the two actions that most directly save lives.

SLIDE 4 | Adult CPR Technique
For adult CPR: confirm the scene is safe, check for responsiveness (tap shoulders and
shout), call 911 or send someone to do so, and retrieve an AED if available. Place
the heel of one hand on the center of the chest (lower half of sternum), place the
other hand on top, and compress at least two inches deep at a rate of 100-120 per
minute. Allow full chest recoil between compressions. Minimize interruptions; limit
pauses to less than 10 seconds.
NARRATION: Push hard and fast with full chest recoil; rate of 100-120 per minute is
roughly the tempo of the song Stayin' Alive by the Bee Gees.

SLIDE 5 | Rescue Breaths and Compression-Only CPR
If trained and willing, give rescue breaths: tilt the head back, lift the chin, pinch
the nose, and seal your mouth over the victim's. Give one breath over one second,
watch for chest rise, then give the second breath. With rescue breaths, the ratio is
30 compressions to 2 breaths. If you are unwilling or unable to give rescue breaths,
hands-only CPR (compression-only) is effective for adult cardiac arrest and recommended
by the AHA. Continue until AED arrives or EMS takes over.
NARRATION: Compression-only CPR is better than no CPR at all; for adults, the oxygen
already in the blood can sustain vital organs during the first minutes of resuscitation.

SLIDE 6 | Child and Infant CPR Differences
For children aged 1 to puberty: use one or two hands for compressions, compress
about two inches deep, and give rescue breaths at a 30:2 ratio for one rescuer or
15:2 for two rescuers. For infants under one year old: use two fingers (or two thumb
encircling technique for two rescuers), compress about 1.5 inches deep over the
center of the chest just below the nipple line. Infant and child cardiac arrest is
more often caused by respiratory failure than heart disease.
NARRATION: Children and infants most often arrest due to breathing problems, not
heart disease; rescue breaths are especially important for pediatric victims.

SLIDE 7 | AED Use
An AED (Automated External Defibrillator) delivers a shock to restore a normal heart
rhythm. Steps: Power on the AED, Attach the pads to bare skin as shown in the diagrams
(one below the right collarbone, one on the left side below the armpit), Analyze
(stand clear, let the AED analyze the heart rhythm), and Shock if advised (stand clear
and press the shock button). Resume CPR immediately after the shock. AEDs are designed
for untrained users and will not shock a normal heart rhythm.
NARRATION: AEDs are safe and easy to use; the machine guides you with voice prompts
and will only shock if it detects a shockable rhythm.

SLIDE 8 | Choking in Adults - Heimlich Maneuver
A choking adult may clutch their throat (universal choking sign), cannot speak, cough,
or breathe effectively. Ask "Are you choking?" If yes and they cannot cough forcefully,
perform abdominal thrusts (Heimlich maneuver): stand behind the victim, make a fist
just above the navel, grasp the fist with your other hand, and give quick upward and
inward thrusts until the object is expelled or the victim loses consciousness.
If conscious, continue thrusts until successful.
NARRATION: If the victim can cough forcefully, encourage them to keep coughing; only
intervene with abdominal thrusts if they cannot cough, speak, or breathe.

SLIDE 9 | Choking in Children and Infants
For a choking child over one year, use the same Heimlich maneuver as for adults but
adjust force proportionally. For a choking infant under one year: hold the infant face-
down on your forearm, give five firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the
heel of your hand, then turn the infant face-up and give five chest thrusts with two
fingers on the center of the chest. Alternate back blows and chest thrusts until the
object is expelled. Never perform blind finger sweeps.
NARRATION: Never do a blind finger sweep in an infant's mouth; you may push the
object deeper into the airway.

SLIDE 10 | Bleeding Control - Direct Pressure
Severe bleeding can cause death within minutes. Apply firm, direct pressure with a
clean cloth or bandage and do not remove it; add more material on top if blood soaks
through. Maintain pressure continuously for at least 10-15 minutes. Elevate the wound
above the heart if possible and if there is no suspected fracture. Pressure dressings
help maintain sustained compression. After stopping severe bleeding, treat for shock
and call 911 if not already done.
NARRATION: Do not remove the blood-soaked cloth; removing it disrupts the clot; add
more material on top and keep pressing firmly.

SLIDE 11 | Tourniquet Application
Tourniquets are used when direct pressure fails to control life-threatening limb
bleeding, such as traumatic amputations. Apply the tourniquet two to three inches
above the wound (not over a joint). Tighten until bleeding stops. Note the time of
application and do not remove the tourniquet in the field. Commercial tourniquets such
as the CAT (Combat Application Tourniquet) are preferred. In an emergency, an
improvised tourniquet can be made from a wide cloth and a stick as a windlass.
NARRATION: Write the time of tourniquet application on the patient or tourniquet so
emergency medical personnel know how long it has been in place.

SLIDE 12 | Shock Recognition and Treatment
Shock occurs when the body cannot circulate enough blood to meet its needs. Signs
include pale, cool, and clammy skin; rapid, weak pulse; rapid breathing; confusion;
and nausea. Causes include severe bleeding, severe allergic reaction, heart failure,
and spinal injury. Treatment: call 911, have the victim lie flat, keep them warm,
elevate legs 8-12 inches if no spinal injury is suspected, and monitor breathing.
Do not give anything to eat or drink.
NARRATION: Shock is a medical emergency even if the person looks alert; the body
is compensating but can decompensate rapidly without treatment.

SLIDE 13 | Burns - Classification and Treatment
First-degree burns affect only the outer skin layer (epidermis); they appear red and
painful, like a sunburn. Second-degree burns affect the dermis; they cause blisters,
intense pain, and moist appearance. Third-degree burns destroy all skin layers and
may appear white, brown, or black with little or no pain due to nerve destruction.
For minor burns, cool with cool running water for 10-20 minutes. Never use ice, butter,
or toothpaste. Cover loosely with a sterile bandage. Seek medical care for burns larger
than three inches, burns on the face, hands, feet, or genitals, or any third-degree burn.
NARRATION: Cool water cools the burn and reduces damage; ice constricts blood vessels
and increases tissue damage.

SLIDE 14 | Fractures and Splinting
A fracture is a broken bone. Signs include pain, swelling, deformity, bruising, and
inability to use the affected limb. Do not attempt to straighten a deformed fracture.
Immobilize the injury in the position found using a splint that extends beyond the
joints above and below the fracture site. Check circulation, sensation, and movement
(CSM) before and after splinting. Open fractures (bone through skin) are at high risk
for infection; cover the wound with a clean dressing.
NARRATION: Splinting an in-place fracture prevents movement that causes additional
tissue damage and pain; always check CSM after applying a splint.

SLIDE 15 | Head and Spinal Injuries
Suspect a spinal injury after any significant trauma to the head, neck, or back, or
any high-energy mechanism (car crash, fall from height, diving accident). Do not move
the victim unless they are in immediate danger. Maintain manual in-line stabilization
of the head and neck. Signs of head injury include confusion, unequal pupils, vomiting,
increasing drowsiness, and clear fluid from nose or ears (sign of skull fracture).
Monitor breathing; airway management takes priority over spinal precautions if needed.
NARRATION: Moving a patient with a spinal injury can cause permanent paralysis; only
move if the scene is unsafe or the airway requires it.

SLIDE 16 | Allergic Reactions and Anaphylaxis
Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening allergic reaction. Triggers include foods
(peanuts, shellfish), insect stings, latex, and medications. Signs include hives,
facial swelling, throat tightening, difficulty breathing, drop in blood pressure, and
loss of consciousness. Epinephrine auto-injectors (EpiPen) are the first-line
treatment; inject into the outer thigh. Call 911 even after epinephrine because
symptoms may return. Have the patient sit upright if breathing is difficult or lie
flat if in shock.
NARRATION: Epinephrine must be given immediately in anaphylaxis; antihistamines like
Benadryl are not fast enough to reverse a severe anaphylactic reaction.

SLIDE 17 | Stroke Recognition - FAST
Stroke occurs when blood flow to part of the brain is interrupted. Use the FAST
acronym: Face drooping (ask the person to smile - is one side drooping?), Arm weakness
(ask them to raise both arms - does one drift down?), Speech difficulty (ask them to
repeat a phrase - is it slurred or strange?), and Time to call 911. Note the time
symptoms began; clot-dissolving medication is most effective within the first few hours.
Act FAST because every minute of untreated stroke destroys about two million brain cells.
NARRATION: Do not give aspirin for stroke; the most common type is ischemic but some
strokes are hemorrhagic, and aspirin would make a bleeding stroke worse.

SLIDE 18 | Heat Emergencies
Heat cramps are painful muscle spasms from excessive sweating and electrolyte loss;
treat with rest, cool environment, and fluid replacement. Heat exhaustion occurs when
the body overheats with heavy sweating, weakness, pale and moist skin, and nausea;
move to a cool place, remove excess clothing, apply cool wet cloths, and give cool
fluids if conscious. Heatstroke is a life-threatening emergency where the body stops
sweating, skin becomes hot and dry or red, and confusion or unconsciousness occurs;
call 911 and cool the victim aggressively with ice water or cool wet towels.
NARRATION: Heatstroke is a true emergency; the core temperature is rising out of
control and aggressive cooling must begin immediately while waiting for EMS.

SLIDE 19 | Hypothermia
Hypothermia occurs when the body loses heat faster than it can produce it, causing
core temperature to drop below 95 degrees F. Signs progress from shivering and
clumsiness to confusion, loss of shivering, and unconsciousness. Remove the victim
from the cold, replace wet clothing with dry insulation, and cover with blankets
including the head. Handle gently to avoid triggering cardiac arrhythmias. Give warm
fluids if the victim is conscious and alert. Call 911 for moderate to severe hypothermia.
NARRATION: Hypothermia victims may appear dead but can survive with proper rewarming;
do not assume death until the victim has been warmed and still shows no signs of life.

SLIDE 20 | Poisoning
Poisoning can occur through ingestion, inhalation, skin contact, or injection. For
ingested poisons, call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222 in the U.S.) or 911 immediately.
Do not induce vomiting unless directed by Poison Control; some substances cause more
damage coming back up. For inhaled poisons (carbon monoxide, fumes), move the victim
to fresh air immediately and call 911. For skin or eye contact with chemicals, flush
with large amounts of cool water for 15-20 minutes.
NARRATION: Poison Control provides free, expert guidance 24 hours a day; always call
them or 911 before giving any treatment for poisoning.

SLIDE 21 | Seizures
A seizure is caused by abnormal electrical activity in the brain. Tonic-clonic (grand
mal) seizures involve muscle stiffening followed by rhythmic jerking. During a seizure:
protect the person from injury by clearing the area, cushion the head, time the
seizure, and position on their side after jerking stops to prevent choking. Do not
restrain movement, do not put anything in the mouth, and do not give water or food
until fully alert. Call 911 if the seizure lasts more than five minutes, another
follows, or the person does not regain consciousness.
NARRATION: Never put anything in a seizing person's mouth; contrary to myth, it is
impossible to swallow your tongue during a seizure.

SLIDE 22 | Diabetic Emergencies
Hypoglycemia (low blood sugar) occurs when blood sugar drops too low, causing
shakiness, sweating, confusion, and weakness. If the victim is conscious and able to
swallow, give 15-20 grams of fast-acting carbohydrates (glucose tablets, juice, or
regular soda). Wait 15 minutes and re-check; if no improvement, repeat. Hyperglycemia
(high blood sugar) develops gradually and causes increased thirst, urination, fruity
breath, and fatigue. Hyperglycemia requires medical treatment; call 911 for any
unconscious diabetic patient.
NARRATION: When unsure whether a diabetic emergency is high or low blood sugar, give
sugar anyway; it helps a hypoglycemic patient and does not significantly harm a
hyperglycemic one.

SLIDE 23 | Consent and Good Samaritan Laws
Before providing first aid to a conscious adult, obtain expressed consent by identifying
yourself, explaining what you will do, and asking permission. Consent is implied for
unconscious victims, children when parents cannot be reached, and victims who cannot
respond. Good Samaritan laws protect bystanders who provide reasonable assistance in
good faith from legal liability in most states. These laws typically require that help
be given voluntarily, without gross negligence, and without expectation of payment.
NARRATION: Good Samaritan protection encourages bystanders to help; acting is almost
always legally protected as long as you act reasonably and in good faith.

SLIDE 24 | CPR for Special Circumstances
For a drowning victim, begin CPR with rescue breaths first (five initial breaths) then
follow standard 30:2 CPR. For a victim in a wheelchair, transfer to a firm, flat
surface before compressions if possible. For a pregnant victim, use standard CPR but
tilt the patient slightly to the left to relieve pressure on the vena cava from the
uterus. For an AED, do not use on infants under one year (unless pediatric pads are
available); remove a medication patch from the chest before applying AED pads.
NARRATION: Pregnancy CPR uses the same technique but shift the patient left; this
prevents the uterus from compressing the vein returning blood to the heart.

SLIDE 25 | Certification Renewal Requirements
CPR and first aid certifications from the American Heart Association and American Red
Cross are valid for two years. Renewal requires a refresher course, which is typically
shorter than the initial course. Healthcare providers follow AHA BLS (Basic Life Support)
standards, which include two-rescuer CPR, bag-mask ventilation, and AED use with
advanced integration. Some workplaces require annual recertification or skills checks
to maintain competency. Keep your card accessible and check the expiration date.
NARRATION: Skills decay without practice; many certified providers also attend annual
skills drills even when not required for recertification.

SLIDE 26 | Putting It All Together - Scene Safety and Priority of Care
Before approaching any emergency: ensure scene safety (look for traffic, fire, electrical
hazards, or violence). If the scene is unsafe, do not enter; call 911 and wait for
responders. Priority of care when multiple victims are present: those with life-
threatening conditions (cardiac arrest, severe bleeding) come first. Always protect
yourself with PPE (gloves and face shield) when available. After every emergency, seek
support; witnessing or responding to traumatic events can have lasting psychological
effects.
NARRATION: Scene safety is always step one; a rescuer who becomes a second victim
reduces the help available to the original victim.
