LESSON: Calculus I
LANGUAGE: en

SLIDE 1 | Welcome to Calculus I
Calculus is the mathematics of change and accumulation. Calculus I focuses on two big ideas:
the derivative, which measures how fast something changes, and the integral, which measures
how much accumulates. Both ideas grow from the single concept of a limit.
NARRATION: Calculus studies change and accumulation, and everything in it flows from the idea of a limit.

SLIDE 2 | Limits Intuitively
The limit of a function f(x) as x approaches a is the value f(x) gets closer and closer to,
even if f(a) itself is undefined. We write this as lim_{x→a} f(x) = L. Limits let us talk
rigorously about instantaneous rates and areas under curves.
NARRATION: A limit asks what value a function approaches as the input gets closer and closer to a target.

SLIDE 3 | Limit Laws
If the limits of f and g both exist as x approaches a, then the limit of their sum, difference,
product, or quotient (when the denominator's limit is nonzero) equals the corresponding
operation on the individual limits. These laws let you evaluate most limits by substitution.
NARRATION: Limit laws let you break a complicated limit into simpler pieces you can evaluate separately.

SLIDE 4 | Computing Limits — Algebraic Techniques
When direct substitution produces 0/0 (an indeterminate form), try factoring and canceling,
rationalizing with a conjugate, or applying a known trigonometric limit such as
lim_{x→0} (sin x)/x = 1. Simplify the expression first, then substitute.
NARRATION: Factor, cancel, or use known special limits to resolve indeterminate zero-over-zero forms.

SLIDE 5 | One-Sided Limits
The left-hand limit lim_{x→a⁻} f(x) considers only x-values approaching a from below, and the
right-hand limit lim_{x→a⁺} considers only x-values from above. The two-sided limit exists if
and only if both one-sided limits exist and are equal.
NARRATION: The two-sided limit exists only when the function approaches the same value from both directions.

SLIDE 6 | Limits at Infinity
As x → +∞ or x → −∞, we ask what value (if any) f(x) approaches. If lim_{x→∞} f(x) = L,
then y = L is a horizontal asymptote. For rational functions, divide every term by the highest
power of x in the denominator to find the limit at infinity.
NARRATION: Horizontal asymptotes come from limits at infinity, found by comparing the degrees of numerator and denominator.

SLIDE 7 | Continuity
A function f is continuous at x = a if f(a) is defined, lim_{x→a} f(x) exists, and the limit
equals f(a). There are three main types of discontinuity: removable (a hole), jump (the two
one-sided limits differ), and infinite (the function blows up to ±∞).
NARRATION: Continuity means no holes, no jumps, and no blow-ups — the graph can be drawn without lifting your pencil.

SLIDE 8 | The Definition of the Derivative
The derivative of f at x is defined as f'(x) = lim_{h→0} [f(x+h) − f(x)] / h, the limit of
the difference quotient. Geometrically it gives the slope of the tangent line to the graph at
that point, and physically it gives the instantaneous rate of change.
NARRATION: The derivative is the limit of the slope of secant lines as the two points get infinitely close together.

SLIDE 9 | Derivative Notation
Leibniz notation writes the derivative as dy/dx or d/dx[f(x)], emphasizing the ratio of
infinitesimal changes. Prime notation writes f'(x) or y'. Higher derivatives are written
f''(x), f'''(x), or d²y/dx². All notations describe the same object.
NARRATION: dy/dx and f-prime-of-x both mean the derivative; choose whichever notation fits the context.

SLIDE 10 | The Power Rule
For any real constant n, the derivative of x^n is nx^{n−1}. This single rule handles polynomials,
roots (written as fractional exponents), and reciprocals (written as negative exponents). For
example, the derivative of x³ is 3x², and the derivative of √x = x^{1/2} is (1/2)x^{-1/2}.
NARRATION: Bring the exponent down as a coefficient and reduce the exponent by one — that is the power rule.

SLIDE 11 | The Product and Quotient Rules
The product rule states (fg)' = f'g + fg'. The quotient rule states (f/g)' = (f'g − fg') / g².
Remember the quotient rule with the phrase "low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared."
Both rules are derived from the limit definition of the derivative.
NARRATION: The product rule adds two terms; the quotient rule subtracts them and divides by the denominator squared.

SLIDE 12 | The Chain Rule
If y = f(g(x)), then dy/dx = f'(g(x)) · g'(x). The chain rule says: differentiate the outside
function (leaving the inside alone), then multiply by the derivative of the inside. For
example, d/dx[sin(x²)] = cos(x²) · 2x.
NARRATION: The chain rule multiplies the derivative of the outer function by the derivative of the inner function.

SLIDE 13 | Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions
The basic trig derivatives are d/dx[sin x] = cos x and d/dx[cos x] = −sin x. From these,
using the quotient rule, d/dx[tan x] = sec²x, d/dx[cot x] = −csc²x,
d/dx[sec x] = sec x tan x, and d/dx[csc x] = −csc x cot x.
NARRATION: Sine goes to cosine; cosine goes to negative sine — the other four follow from the quotient rule.

SLIDE 14 | Derivatives of Exponential and Logarithmic Functions
The natural exponential function has the remarkable property d/dx[eˣ] = eˣ: it equals its own
derivative. The natural logarithm satisfies d/dx[ln x] = 1/x. For other bases,
d/dx[aˣ] = aˣ ln a and d/dx[log_a x] = 1/(x ln a).
NARRATION: e to the x is its own derivative, and the derivative of ln x is simply one over x.

SLIDE 15 | Implicit Differentiation
When a relation between x and y is given implicitly (not solved for y), differentiate both sides
with respect to x, treating y as a function of x and applying the chain rule to every term
involving y, then solve the resulting equation for dy/dx.
NARRATION: Implicit differentiation lets you find dy/dx even when y is tangled together with x in one equation.

SLIDE 16 | Related Rates
In a related-rates problem, two or more quantities change with time and are related by an
equation. Differentiate that equation implicitly with respect to time t, substitute known rates
and values, and solve for the unknown rate. Always define variables and draw a diagram first.
NARRATION: Related rates use implicit differentiation with respect to time to connect the rates at which quantities change.

SLIDE 17 | Absolute and Local Extrema
A function has a local (relative) maximum or minimum at a point where it changes from
increasing to decreasing or vice versa. An absolute extremum is the largest or smallest value
over a given interval. Candidates for absolute extrema are critical points and endpoints.
NARRATION: Extrema occur at critical points where the derivative is zero or undefined, and at the endpoints of closed intervals.

SLIDE 18 | The Mean Value Theorem
If f is continuous on [a, b] and differentiable on (a, b), then there exists at least one c in
(a, b) where f'(c) = [f(b) − f(a)] / (b − a). The MVT says the instantaneous rate of change
must equal the average rate of change at some interior point.
NARRATION: The Mean Value Theorem guarantees a point where the tangent line is parallel to the secant line across the interval.

SLIDE 19 | First Derivative Test
Find all critical numbers where f'(x) = 0 or f'(x) is undefined. If f' changes from positive
to negative at a critical number, that point is a local maximum. If f' changes from negative
to positive, it is a local minimum. If f' does not change sign, it is neither.
NARRATION: The first derivative test classifies critical points by watching whether the derivative changes sign.

SLIDE 20 | Concavity and the Second Derivative Test
f is concave up where f'' > 0 and concave down where f'' < 0. A point where concavity changes
is an inflection point. The second derivative test says: if f'(c) = 0 and f''(c) > 0, then c
is a local minimum; if f''(c) < 0, it is a local maximum.
NARRATION: A positive second derivative means concave up (like a cup); negative means concave down (like a cap).

SLIDE 21 | L'Hopital's Rule
If lim_{x→a} f(x)/g(x) produces an indeterminate form 0/0 or ∞/∞, then under mild conditions
lim_{x→a} f(x)/g(x) = lim_{x→a} f'(x)/g'(x). Differentiate numerator and denominator
separately (not using the quotient rule) and take the limit again.
NARRATION: L'Hopital's rule replaces an indeterminate limit with the limit of the ratio of derivatives.

SLIDE 22 | Optimization Problems
To optimize a quantity: (1) write a primary equation for what you want to maximize or minimize,
(2) use a constraint to eliminate a variable, (3) differentiate and find critical points, and
(4) verify you have a maximum or minimum using the first or second derivative test.
NARRATION: Optimization means translating a word problem into a function and then finding where its derivative is zero.

SLIDE 23 | Antiderivatives and Indefinite Integrals
An antiderivative of f is any function F such that F'(x) = f(x). The indefinite integral
∫f(x)dx = F(x) + C represents the entire family of antiderivatives, where C is the constant of
integration. For example, ∫x² dx = x³/3 + C.
NARRATION: An antiderivative reverses differentiation, and the plus C accounts for any constant that differentiation erases.

SLIDE 24 | Riemann Sums and the Definite Integral
The definite integral ∫_a^b f(x)dx is defined as the limit of Riemann sums: divide [a, b] into
n subintervals, evaluate f at a sample point in each, multiply by the subinterval width, and
sum as n → ∞. Geometrically this gives the net signed area between f and the x-axis.
NARRATION: A definite integral is the limit of a sum of thin rectangles as the rectangles become infinitely narrow.

SLIDE 25 | The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
Part 1: If F(x) = ∫_a^x f(t)dt, then F'(x) = f(x) — differentiation undoes integration.
Part 2: ∫_a^b f(x)dx = F(b) − F(a) for any antiderivative F of f. Together these two parts
reveal that differentiation and integration are inverse processes.
NARRATION: The Fundamental Theorem is the deepest result in calculus: it shows that derivatives and integrals are inverses.

SLIDE 26 | Curve Sketching with Calculus
A complete curve sketch uses: the domain, intercepts, symmetry, asymptotes (from limits),
intervals of increase/decrease (from f'), local extrema, concavity (from f''), and inflection
points. Calculus transforms curve sketching from guesswork into systematic analysis.
NARRATION: By combining first and second derivative information, you can sketch any differentiable function accurately.

FACT: The derivative measures the instantaneous rate of change of a function.
FACT: The chain rule applies whenever you differentiate a composition of functions.
FACT: L'Hopital's rule handles 0/0 and infinity/infinity indeterminate forms.
FACT: The Fundamental Theorem connects differentiation and integration as inverse operations.
FACT: Concavity is determined by the sign of the second derivative.
FACT: The constant of integration C appears in every indefinite integral.
FACT: The Mean Value Theorem guarantees a tangent line parallel to the secant line somewhere in the interval.
