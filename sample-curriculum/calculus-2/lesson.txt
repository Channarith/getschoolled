LESSON: Calculus II
LANGUAGE: en

SLIDE 1 | Welcome to Calculus II
Calculus II extends the ideas of Calculus I into more advanced integration techniques, infinite
series, and new coordinate systems. By the end you will be able to compute complex integrals,
test whether infinite sums converge, and model curves with parametric and polar equations.
NARRATION: Calculus II deepens integration skills and explores infinite series, parametric equations, and polar coordinates.

SLIDE 2 | Review of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
The Fundamental Theorem links differentiation and integration. Part 1 says d/dx[∫_a^x f(t)dt]
= f(x). Part 2 says ∫_a^b f(x)dx = F(b) − F(a), where F is any antiderivative of f. Every
integration technique in this course ultimately relies on this bridge between the two operations.
NARRATION: The Fundamental Theorem lets us evaluate definite integrals by finding antiderivatives instead of computing limit sums.

SLIDE 3 | U-Substitution
U-substitution reverses the chain rule. Set u equal to an inside function, compute du, rewrite
the entire integral in terms of u, integrate, then substitute back. For definite integrals you
can change the limits to u-limits and avoid back-substitution.
NARRATION: U-substitution is the most fundamental integration technique; always look for a function and its derivative inside the integral.

SLIDE 4 | Integration by Parts
Integration by parts follows the formula ∫u dv = uv − ∫v du, derived from the product rule.
Choose u as the function that simplifies when differentiated (the LIATE priority order helps:
Logarithm, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trigonometric, Exponential).
NARRATION: Integration by parts trades a hard integral for a simpler one by splitting the integrand into two factors.

SLIDE 5 | Trigonometric Integrals
Integrals of the form ∫sinⁿx cosᵐx dx use Pythagorean identities to reduce odd powers. When
both powers are even, use the half-angle identities sin²x = (1 − cos 2x)/2 and
cos²x = (1 + cos 2x)/2 to lower the degrees before integrating.
NARRATION: Strip off one factor to use a Pythagorean identity, or use half-angle identities to reduce even powers.

SLIDE 6 | Trigonometric Substitution
For integrands containing √(a²−x²), √(a²+x²), or √(x²−a²), substitute x = a sin θ,
x = a tan θ, or x = a sec θ respectively. These substitutions remove the radical and replace
it with a simpler trig expression that can be integrated.
NARRATION: Trigonometric substitution eliminates square roots by replacing the variable with an appropriate trig function.

SLIDE 7 | Partial Fraction Decomposition
Any proper rational function (degree of numerator less than denominator) can be written as a
sum of simpler fractions called partial fractions. Factor the denominator completely into linear
and irreducible quadratic factors, set up the decomposition, and solve for the constants.
NARRATION: Partial fractions break a complicated rational function into simple pieces that are easy to integrate individually.

SLIDE 8 | Improper Integrals
An integral is improper if the interval is infinite or if the integrand has a vertical asymptote
inside the interval. Evaluate by replacing the problem endpoint with a limit variable and
computing the limiting value. If the limit exists and is finite, the integral converges.
NARRATION: Improper integrals replace infinity or a discontinuity with a limit and ask whether that limit is finite.

SLIDE 9 | Area Between Curves
The area between y = f(x) and y = g(x) on [a, b] (with f ≥ g) is ∫_a^b [f(x) − g(x)] dx.
Find the intersection points to determine the limits of integration. When curves cross, split the
integral at each crossing and take the absolute difference in each piece.
NARRATION: Subtract the lower curve from the upper curve and integrate to find the area trapped between them.

SLIDE 10 | Volume by Disk and Washer Methods
When a region is rotated about a horizontal or vertical axis, the volume can be found by the
disk method: V = π∫[R(x)]² dx, or the washer method when there is a hole:
V = π∫([R(x)]² − [r(x)]²) dx, where R and r are the outer and inner radii.
NARRATION: Slice the solid into thin disks or washers, compute each circular cross-section's area, and integrate along the axis.

SLIDE 11 | Volume by the Shell Method
The shell method integrates cylindrical shells instead of disks. For rotation about the y-axis,
V = 2π∫x f(x) dx. The shell method is often simpler when the washer method would require
solving for x in terms of y. Choose the method that gives simpler limits and integrand.
NARRATION: The shell method wraps thin cylindrical shells around the axis and integrates their volumes.

SLIDE 12 | Arc Length
The length of a curve y = f(x) from x = a to x = b is L = ∫_a^b √(1 + [f'(x)]²) dx.
This formula comes from summing the lengths of tiny hypotenuses along the curve. For parametric
curves, replace ds with √((dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²) dt.
NARRATION: Arc length integrates infinitesimal hypotenuses along the curve using the Pythagorean theorem.

SLIDE 13 | Work and Fluid Force
Work done by a variable force F(x) over a displacement from a to b is W = ∫_a^b F(x) dx.
Fluid force on a submerged plate uses F = ρg∫ depth · width dy, where ρ is the fluid density.
Both problems reduce to setting up and evaluating a definite integral with a physical meaning.
NARRATION: Physics applications of integration compute work from a variable force and fluid pressure from a submerged surface.

SLIDE 14 | Sequences
A sequence is an ordered list of numbers {a_n}. A sequence converges if lim_{n→∞} a_n = L
for some finite L; otherwise it diverges. Squeeze theorem, L'Hopital's rule (applied to the
continuous version), and geometric sequence formulas are the main convergence tools.
NARRATION: A sequence converges if its terms approach a single finite value as n goes to infinity.

SLIDE 15 | Series and the Concept of Convergence
An infinite series is the sum ∑_{n=1}^{∞} a_n = lim_{N→∞} S_N, where S_N = a_1 + … + a_N
is the Nth partial sum. If this limit exists and is finite, the series converges to that value.
The simplest test: if lim_{n→∞} a_n ≠ 0, the series must diverge (Divergence Test).
NARRATION: A series converges when its partial sums approach a finite limit; if the terms don't go to zero, it diverges immediately.

SLIDE 16 | Geometric Series
A geometric series has the form ∑ar^n. It converges if and only if |r| < 1, and its sum is
a/(1 − r). Geometric series appear in finance (present value calculations), probability, and
as building blocks for more complex power series.
NARRATION: A geometric series converges to a over one minus r whenever the common ratio r has absolute value less than one.

SLIDE 17 | The Integral Test
If f is positive, continuous, and decreasing, then ∑f(n) and ∫f(x)dx either both converge
or both diverge. The p-series ∑1/n^p converges if p > 1 and diverges if p ≤ 1. The integral
test gives a rigorous connection between series and improper integrals.
NARRATION: The integral test compares a series to an improper integral; they share the same convergence behavior.

SLIDE 18 | Comparison Tests
The Direct Comparison Test says: if 0 ≤ a_n ≤ b_n and ∑b_n converges, then ∑a_n converges;
if ∑a_n diverges, so does ∑b_n. The Limit Comparison Test compares lim a_n/b_n to a positive
constant, then concludes both series share the same convergence behavior.
NARRATION: Comparison tests bound an unknown series between known convergent or divergent ones to determine its fate.

SLIDE 19 | Alternating Series and Absolute Convergence
An alternating series ∑(−1)^n b_n converges if b_n is decreasing and b_n → 0. A series is
absolutely convergent if ∑|a_n| converges, and conditionally convergent if it converges but
not absolutely. Absolute convergence implies convergence, but not vice versa.
NARRATION: An alternating series converges when its terms decrease in absolute value to zero, even if it's not absolutely convergent.

SLIDE 20 | Ratio and Root Tests
The Ratio Test computes L = lim |a_{n+1}/a_n|: if L < 1 the series converges absolutely;
if L > 1 it diverges. The Root Test computes L = lim |a_n|^{1/n} and applies the same
criteria. Both tests are especially powerful for series involving factorials or exponentials.
NARRATION: The Ratio Test divides consecutive terms; the Root Test takes nth roots — both look for a limit less than one.

SLIDE 21 | Power Series and Radius of Convergence
A power series is ∑c_n(x − a)^n. It converges absolutely inside an interval (a − R, a + R)
where R is the radius of convergence, found by the Ratio or Root Test on the coefficients.
At the endpoints you must test separately using other convergence tests.
NARRATION: A power series converges inside a symmetric interval around its center; the radius of that interval is R.

SLIDE 22 | Taylor and Maclaurin Series
The Taylor series of f centered at a is ∑[f^{(n)}(a)/n!](x − a)^n. Centered at 0 it is called
a Maclaurin series. Key examples: e^x = ∑x^n/n!, sin x = ∑(−1)^n x^{2n+1}/(2n+1)!,
cos x = ∑(−1)^n x^{2n}/(2n)!, and 1/(1−x) = ∑x^n.
NARRATION: A Taylor series represents a function as an infinite polynomial whose coefficients encode the function's derivatives at a point.

SLIDE 23 | Parametric Equations
A curve is described parametrically by x = f(t), y = g(t). The slope of the tangent line is
dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt). The arc length of a parametric curve is ∫√((dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²) dt.
Parametric equations can represent curves that are not functions of x alone.
NARRATION: Parametric equations trace a curve by giving x and y separately as functions of a third parameter t.

SLIDE 24 | Polar Curves and Area
In polar coordinates, r = f(θ) describes a curve. The area enclosed by a polar curve from
θ = α to θ = β is A = (1/2)∫_α^β [f(θ)]² dθ. For the area between two polar curves use
(1/2)∫[r_outer² − r_inner²] dθ. Common shapes include roses, limacons, and the cardioid.
NARRATION: Polar area sweeps out pie-slice sectors whose areas are integrated over the angular variable theta.

SLIDE 25 | Introduction to Differential Equations — Separable Type
A differential equation involves an unknown function and its derivatives. A separable equation
can be written as dy/dx = g(x)h(y). Separate variables — all y terms on one side, all x terms
on the other — then integrate both sides and solve for y with an arbitrary constant C.
NARRATION: Separate the variables, integrate both sides, and solve for y to find the general solution of a separable equation.

SLIDE 26 | Applications of Differential Equations
Separable differential equations model exponential growth and decay (dP/dt = kP), Newton's law
of cooling, and simple mixing problems. The general solution is P = P_0 e^{kt}; k > 0 gives
growth and k < 0 gives decay. An initial condition pins down the particular solution.
NARRATION: Exponential growth and decay are modeled by the simple separable equation dy/dt equals k times y.

FACT: Integration by parts follows the formula u dv = uv minus the integral of v du.
FACT: A geometric series with |r| < 1 sums to a divided by one minus r.
FACT: The p-series converges when p is greater than one and diverges when p is one or less.
FACT: The Ratio Test checks whether the limit of consecutive term ratios is less than one.
FACT: A Taylor series centered at zero is called a Maclaurin series.
FACT: Polar area uses one-half the integral of r-squared with respect to theta.
FACT: Separable differential equations are solved by moving all y terms to one side and all x terms to the other before integrating.
