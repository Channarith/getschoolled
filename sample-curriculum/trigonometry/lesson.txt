LESSON: Trigonometry
LANGUAGE: en

SLIDE 1 | Welcome to Trigonometry
Trigonometry is the branch of mathematics that studies relationships between the sides and
angles of triangles. It extends far beyond triangles, powering everything from engineering
and navigation to music, light waves, and computer graphics.
NARRATION: Trigonometry connects angles and lengths, and its tools appear everywhere in science and technology.

SLIDE 2 | Angles in Standard Position
An angle is in standard position when its vertex sits at the origin and its initial side lies
along the positive x-axis. The terminal side rotates counterclockwise for positive angles and
clockwise for negative angles.
NARRATION: Place the angle at the origin with its starting ray on the positive x-axis, then rotate to form the angle.

SLIDE 3 | Degree and Radian Measure
Degrees divide a full rotation into 360 equal parts. Radians measure angles by the arc length
on a unit circle, so a full rotation equals 2π radians. To convert, use the fact that π radians
equals 180 degrees.
NARRATION: Radians relate an angle to arc length; pi radians is 180 degrees.

SLIDE 4 | Arc Length and Sector Area
For a circle of radius r, the arc length s subtended by a central angle θ (in radians) is
s = rθ. The area of the corresponding sector is A = (1/2)r²θ. Both formulas require the
angle to be in radians.
NARRATION: Arc length equals radius times angle in radians, and sector area is one-half r-squared theta.

SLIDE 5 | The Unit Circle
The unit circle is a circle of radius 1 centered at the origin. Any point on it has coordinates
(cos θ, sin θ), where θ is the angle in standard position. Memorizing key points on the unit
circle is the foundation of trigonometry.
NARRATION: Every point on the unit circle gives you the cosine and sine of the corresponding angle directly.

SLIDE 6 | The Six Trigonometric Functions
For an angle θ in standard position with a point (x, y) on its terminal side at distance r from
the origin: sin θ = y/r, cos θ = x/r, tan θ = y/x. The reciprocals are csc θ = r/y,
sec θ = r/x, and cot θ = x/y.
NARRATION: Sine, cosine, and tangent are the core three; cosecant, secant, and cotangent are their reciprocals.

SLIDE 7 | Trig Values of Special Angles
For 0°, 30°, 45°, 60°, and 90° (and their radian equivalents 0, π/6, π/4, π/3, π/2), the
sine and cosine values follow a pattern: sin values are √0/2, √1/2, √2/2, √3/2, √4/2.
Knowing these by heart makes calculations fast and reliable.
NARRATION: The special angle values follow a neat square-root pattern that is worth memorizing.

SLIDE 8 | Reference Angles
The reference angle is the acute angle formed between the terminal side and the x-axis. It is
always positive and between 0° and 90°. You find the trig value of any angle by computing the
trig value of its reference angle and attaching the correct sign based on the quadrant.
NARRATION: Find the reference angle first, then adjust the sign according to which quadrant you are in.

SLIDE 9 | Signs in the Four Quadrants — ASTC
In Quadrant I all six functions are positive. In Quadrant II only sine (and cosecant) are
positive. In Quadrant III only tangent (and cotangent) are positive. In Quadrant IV only
cosine (and secant) are positive. The mnemonic "All Students Take Calculus" helps.
NARRATION: Remember "All Students Take Calculus" to recall which functions are positive in each quadrant.

SLIDE 10 | Graphing the Sine Function
The graph of y = sin x is a smooth wave that passes through the origin, reaches a maximum of 1
at x = π/2, returns to 0 at x = π, falls to −1 at x = 3π/2, and completes one full cycle at
x = 2π. The pattern repeats forever in both directions.
NARRATION: Sine starts at zero, rises to one, falls to negative one, and returns to zero over one full period of 2π.

SLIDE 11 | Graphing the Cosine Function
The graph of y = cos x looks identical to sine but shifted left by π/2. It starts at 1 when
x = 0, falls to 0 at x = π/2, reaches −1 at x = π, and returns to 1 at x = 2π. Both sine
and cosine have period 2π and range [−1, 1].
NARRATION: Cosine starts at its maximum of one and has the same wave shape as sine, shifted left by a quarter period.

SLIDE 12 | Amplitude and Period
For y = A sin(Bx) or y = A cos(Bx), the amplitude is |A|, which is the distance from the
midline to the peak. The period is 2π/|B|, the length of one complete cycle. Changing A
stretches or compresses the graph vertically; changing B stretches or compresses it horizontally.
NARRATION: Amplitude sets the height of the wave; period sets how long one full cycle takes.

SLIDE 13 | Phase Shift and Vertical Shift
The full form y = A sin(B(x − C)) + D shifts the graph horizontally by C units (right if C > 0)
and vertically by D units. C is the phase shift and D is the vertical shift (midline). Together
these four parameters give complete control over the sine or cosine wave.
NARRATION: The phase shift moves the wave left or right, and the vertical shift moves the midline up or down.

SLIDE 14 | Graphing the Tangent Function
The graph of y = tan x has vertical asymptotes wherever cos x = 0, that is at x = π/2 + nπ.
Between consecutive asymptotes, the function increases from −∞ to +∞. The period of tangent is
π, half that of sine and cosine.
NARRATION: Tangent has a period of pi and shoots to infinity at each vertical asymptote where cosine is zero.

SLIDE 15 | Reciprocal Functions: Cosecant, Secant, Cotangent
The graphs of csc x and sec x have U-shaped arches (called hyperbola-like curves) that touch
the sine and cosine graphs at their peaks and troughs. Cotangent is a decreasing curve with
period π, mirroring tangent. All three are undefined wherever their denominators equal zero.
NARRATION: Cosecant and secant have arch-shaped graphs that exist only where sine and cosine are nonzero.

SLIDE 16 | Inverse Trigonometric Functions
The inverse functions arcsin, arccos, and arctan undo the trig functions. Because sine and
cosine are not one-to-one over all real numbers, their inverses are defined on restricted
domains: arcsin on [−π/2, π/2], arccos on [0, π], and arctan on (−π/2, π/2).
NARRATION: Inverse trig functions give you the angle when you know the ratio, using carefully restricted domains.

SLIDE 17 | Pythagorean Identities
The fundamental Pythagorean identity is sin²θ + cos²θ = 1. Dividing through by cos²θ gives
tan²θ + 1 = sec²θ, and dividing by sin²θ gives 1 + cot²θ = csc²θ. These three identities are
the backbone of trigonometric simplification and proof.
NARRATION: Sin squared plus cos squared equals one — this identity and its two siblings are essential tools.

SLIDE 18 | Quotient and Reciprocal Identities
The quotient identities state that tan θ = sin θ / cos θ and cot θ = cos θ / sin θ. The
reciprocal identities define csc θ = 1/sin θ, sec θ = 1/cos θ, and cot θ = 1/tan θ. These
definitions let you rewrite any trig expression in terms of sine and cosine.
NARRATION: Tangent is sine over cosine, and each of the other functions is a simple ratio or reciprocal.

SLIDE 19 | Sum and Difference Identities
The sum formulas are sin(A ± B) = sin A cos B ± cos A sin B and
cos(A ± B) = cos A cos B ∓ sin A sin B. These allow you to evaluate trig functions at angles
not on the standard list by splitting them into sums of familiar angles.
NARRATION: Sum and difference identities let you evaluate angles like 75 degrees by splitting them into 45 plus 30.

SLIDE 20 | Double-Angle and Half-Angle Formulas
The double-angle formulas are sin 2θ = 2 sin θ cos θ and cos 2θ = cos²θ − sin²θ (with two
equivalent alternate forms). The half-angle formulas express sin(θ/2) and cos(θ/2) in terms
of cos θ. These are derived directly from the sum identities.
NARRATION: Double-angle formulas compress two-angle expressions; half-angle formulas are their inverse.

SLIDE 21 | Solving Trigonometric Equations
To solve equations like sin x = 1/2, first find the reference angle, then list all angles in
[0, 2π) that satisfy the equation, and finally add the general period (2πn or πn for tangent)
for the complete infinite solution set. Always check that solutions fit any stated domain.
NARRATION: Find reference angles, identify all solutions in one period, then write the general solution with the period added.

SLIDE 22 | The Law of Sines
In any triangle with sides a, b, c opposite angles A, B, C respectively, the Law of Sines
states a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C. It is used to solve triangles when you know two angles and
a side (AAS or ASA) or two sides and a non-included angle (SSA, the ambiguous case).
NARRATION: The Law of Sines links each side to the sine of its opposite angle in a constant ratio.

SLIDE 23 | The Law of Cosines
The Law of Cosines generalizes the Pythagorean theorem: c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C. Use it
when you know three sides (SSS) or two sides and the included angle (SAS). When C = 90°,
the formula reduces exactly to the Pythagorean theorem.
NARRATION: The Law of Cosines is the Pythagorean theorem extended to any triangle, not just right triangles.

SLIDE 24 | Area Formulas Using Trigonometry
The area of a triangle with two sides a, b and included angle C is K = (1/2)ab sin C. This
formula works even when the height is not given directly. Combined with Heron's formula, it
gives flexible tools for computing triangle area from various combinations of known parts.
NARRATION: Multiply one-half times two sides times the sine of the included angle to get a triangle's area.

SLIDE 25 | Polar Coordinates
In polar coordinates a point is described by (r, θ), where r is the distance from the origin
and θ is the angle from the positive x-axis. The conversion relations are x = r cos θ and
y = r sin θ, with r² = x² + y² and θ = arctan(y/x) adjusted for the correct quadrant.
NARRATION: Polar coordinates describe a point by its distance and direction rather than horizontal and vertical position.

SLIDE 26 | De Moivre's Theorem
De Moivre's theorem states that (r(cos θ + i sin θ))ⁿ = rⁿ(cos nθ + i sin nθ). Written in
exponential form using Euler's formula, this becomes (re^{iθ})ⁿ = rⁿe^{inθ}. It is a powerful
tool for computing powers and roots of complex numbers.
NARRATION: De Moivre's theorem lets you raise a complex number in polar form to any power by multiplying the angle.

SLIDE 27 | Real-World Applications of Trigonometry
Trigonometry models periodic phenomena: sound and light waves use sine functions, engineers
analyze alternating current with amplitude and phase shift, and navigators use the law of sines
and cosines to plot courses. Architects apply trig to calculate roof pitches and structural loads.
NARRATION: From ocean waves to GPS navigation, trigonometry is the mathematical language of cycles and directions.

FACT: One full rotation equals 2π radians or 360 degrees.
FACT: The unit circle has radius 1 and gives cos and sin for every angle.
FACT: sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 is the fundamental Pythagorean identity.
FACT: The period of sine and cosine is 2π; the period of tangent is π.
FACT: The Law of Cosines reduces to the Pythagorean theorem when the angle is 90 degrees.
FACT: De Moivre's theorem connects complex number exponentiation with angle multiplication.
FACT: Amplitude is the absolute value of the coefficient A in y = A sin(Bx).
