LESSON: Pharmacy Technician Certification Prep (PTCE/ExCPT)
LANGUAGE: en

SLIDE 1 | Role of the Pharmacy Technician
Pharmacy technicians assist pharmacists in dispensing medications, managing inventory,
processing prescriptions, and serving patients. Technicians may work in retail,
hospital, long-term care, mail-order, and specialty pharmacy settings. The pharmacist
supervises all technician work and is responsible for final verification. Technicians
must demonstrate accuracy, attention to detail, and strong communication skills.
NARRATION: Pharmacy technicians are essential team members; your accuracy directly
affects patient safety every single day.

SLIDE 2 | Pharmacy Law Overview
Federal pharmacy law establishes the minimum standards; state law may be stricter.
The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FDCA) governs drug safety and labeling.
The Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) regulates controlled substances. The
Combat Methamphetamine Epidemic Act (CMEA) limits pseudoephedrine sales. OBRA 90
(Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act) requires pharmacists to offer counseling to
Medicaid patients. State boards of pharmacy issue licenses and set technician ratios.
NARRATION: Always know which law is more restrictive; when federal and state law
conflict, follow the stricter standard.

SLIDE 3 | Controlled Substance Schedules
The DEA classifies controlled substances into five schedules based on medical use
and abuse potential. Schedule I (e.g., heroin, LSD) has no accepted medical use.
Schedule II (e.g., oxycodone, amphetamine, fentanyl) has high abuse potential with
accepted medical use. Schedule III (e.g., codeine combinations, anabolic steroids)
has moderate potential. Schedule IV (e.g., benzodiazepines, tramadol) has lower
potential. Schedule V (e.g., cough preparations with small amounts of codeine) has
the lowest potential.
NARRATION: Schedule II drugs require the strictest controls; they cannot be refilled
and require a new written or electronic prescription each time.

SLIDE 4 | Prescription Requirements and Processing
A valid prescription must include the patient's name, date written, prescriber's name
and DEA number (for controlled substances), drug name and strength, quantity,
directions for use, and the prescriber's signature. Technicians verify completeness,
enter data into the pharmacy system, retrieve and count the medication, label the
vial, and present to the pharmacist for final verification. Oral prescriptions for
non-controlled drugs may be taken by a technician.
NARRATION: Never dispense a prescription with missing information; always verify
completeness before processing to prevent errors and legal violations.

SLIDE 5 | Dosage Calculations - Ratios and Proportions
Pharmacy calculations use ratio and proportion to find unknown quantities. If a
medication is available as 250 mg per 5 mL, and the dose is 500 mg, set up the
proportion: 250/5 = 500/X. Cross-multiply: 250X = 2500, so X = 10 mL. This method
works for any concentration-based calculation. Always include units in your work
to catch errors, and double-check every calculation.
NARRATION: Setting up proportions correctly and canceling units is the most reliable
way to solve dosage calculations; show your work to catch mistakes.

SLIDE 6 | Dosage Calculations - IV Flow Rates
IV flow rate calculations determine how quickly to infuse an IV solution. The formula
is: mL per hour = (volume in mL / time in hours). For drop rate: drops per minute =
(volume in mL x drop factor in gtt/mL) / time in minutes. Common drop factors are 10,
15, or 60 gtt/mL depending on the tubing type. Infusion pumps handle most calculations
automatically but technicians must verify the programmed rate.
NARRATION: A calculation error on an IV can deliver a fatal dose or fail to treat
a life-threatening condition; verify every IV order twice.

SLIDE 7 | Brand vs Generic Drugs
Generic drugs contain the same active ingredient, strength, dosage form, and route
of administration as the brand-name drug. They must be bioequivalent, meaning they
are absorbed into the bloodstream at the same rate and extent. Generics cost 80-85%
less than brand drugs on average. The Orange Book (FDA publication) lists approved
generic equivalents. Some drugs are dispense-as-written (DAW), meaning the brand
must be dispensed as written by the prescriber.
NARRATION: DAW codes on a prescription tell you whether substitution is allowed;
always check the DAW code before substituting a generic.

SLIDE 8 | Top 200 Drugs Overview
Pharmacy technician exams require familiarity with the most commonly dispensed
medications. Key drug classes include statins (atorvastatin/Lipitor for cholesterol),
ACE inhibitors (lisinopril for blood pressure), beta-blockers (metoprolol for heart
conditions), SSRIs (sertraline/Zoloft for depression), proton pump inhibitors
(omeprazole/Prilosec for acid reflux), metformin (for type 2 diabetes), albuterol
(for asthma), and amoxicillin (antibiotic). Know brand and generic names, common
doses, and drug class.
NARRATION: The top 200 drugs are a foundation of technician knowledge; memorize
the generic name, brand name, and drug class for each.

SLIDE 9 | Drug Interactions and Contraindications
Drug interactions occur when one drug alters the effect of another. Pharmacokinetic
interactions affect absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion. Pharmacodynamic
interactions alter the drug's effect at the target site. Warfarin has hundreds of
clinically significant interactions because it is metabolized by CYP450 enzymes.
Contraindications are conditions in which a drug should not be used, such as ACE
inhibitors in pregnancy. The pharmacist screens for interactions; technicians should
flag unusual combinations.
NARRATION: Drug interaction checking is automated in pharmacy software, but
technicians must understand the concepts to recognize flagged alerts correctly.

SLIDE 10 | Sterile Compounding - Aseptic Technique
Sterile compounding prepares IV admixtures, injections, and ophthalmic solutions
that must be free of microbial contamination. All sterile compounding is done in a
laminar airflow workbench (LAFW) or a biological safety cabinet (BSC) using ISO Class
5 air quality. Technicians must perform hand hygiene, don gown, gloves, mask, and
hair cover before entering the clean room. Swab all injection surfaces with 70%
isopropyl alcohol and allow to dry before use.
NARRATION: One contamination error in sterile compounding can cause a patient to
develop a life-threatening infection; there is zero tolerance for technique shortcuts.

SLIDE 11 | USP 795 and USP 797 Standards
USP 795 governs non-sterile compounding, setting standards for facilities, equipment,
training, and beyond-use dating. USP 797 governs sterile compounding and sets stricter
requirements for clean room design, personnel training, environmental monitoring, and
beyond-use dates based on risk level. Category 1 CSPs (compounded sterile preparations)
have shorter beyond-use dates than Category 2 CSPs made under full USP 797 conditions.
NARRATION: USP 797 compliance is strictly enforced by state boards; failure to comply
can result in pharmacy closure and patient harm.

SLIDE 12 | Non-Sterile Compounding
Non-sterile compounding prepares oral solutions, creams, ointments, suppositories,
and capsules from raw ingredients or commercial products. Technicians must use
calibrated equipment, verified formulas, and appropriate excipients. Beyond-use dates
(BUDs) for non-sterile preparations are shorter than manufactured drug expiration
dates because stability data is limited. Documentation of every compounded preparation
must be kept in a compounding log.
NARRATION: Compounding allows pharmacies to prepare medications not commercially
available, such as custom strengths or flavored pediatric preparations.

SLIDE 13 | Medication Dispensing Errors and Prevention
Common dispensing errors include wrong drug, wrong dose, wrong patient, wrong route,
and wrong time. The Five Rights (Right Patient, Right Drug, Right Dose, Right Route,
Right Time) provide a safety checklist. Bar-code medication administration (BCMA)
systems in hospitals scan both the patient's wristband and the medication before
administration. Look-alike/sound-alike (LASA) drug pairs such as hydroxyzine and
hydralazine require special labeling and storage separation.
NARRATION: LASA drugs cause preventable deaths; separate them in storage and use
tall-man lettering (e.g., hydrOXYzine vs hydrALAzine) to distinguish them.

SLIDE 14 | Point-of-Sale and Insurance Billing
Pharmacy technicians process insurance claims through pharmacy management software
using BIN (Bank Identification Number), PCN (Processor Control Number), and group and
member ID numbers from the patient's insurance card. Rejections may occur for reasons
such as refill too soon, prior authorization required, or formulary exclusion. Technicians
resolve common rejections by overriding refill-too-soon errors (with appropriate
justification) or directing the patient to contact their insurer.
NARRATION: Prior authorization (PA) rejections require the prescriber to justify the
medication to the insurance company; the pharmacy cannot override this on its own.

SLIDE 15 | Inventory Management
Pharmacies use FIFO (First In, First Out) to rotate stock so older products are
dispensed first. Check expiration dates during inventory audits and remove expired
products promptly. Controlled substances require perpetual inventory records updated
with each receipt and dispensing. The pharmacy must conduct a biennial DEA inventory
of all Schedule II substances. Automated dispensing cabinets (ADCs) in hospitals
track controlled substance use electronically.
NARRATION: Controlled substance inventory discrepancies must be investigated and
reported to the DEA if theft or loss is suspected.

SLIDE 16 | Communication with Pharmacists and Patients
Effective communication with supervising pharmacists includes promptly reporting any
prescription discrepancies, patient concerns, or system alerts. With patients, use
clear and respectful language, confirm the patient's identity using name and date of
birth, and direct all clinical questions to the pharmacist. Active listening and
empathy are essential when patients are anxious about medications. Never provide
clinical advice outside your scope of practice.
NARRATION: Your scope of practice does not include counseling patients on drug
therapy; redirect clinical questions to the pharmacist every time.

SLIDE 17 | HIPAA in the Pharmacy
HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) protects patient health
information (PHI). Pharmacies may not share PHI without patient authorization except
for treatment, payment, and health care operations. Prescription information may not
be shared with family members without the patient's consent. Keep computer screens
shielded from public view, speak quietly when discussing patient information, and
shred all paper PHI. HIPAA violations carry civil and criminal penalties.
NARRATION: Never share a patient's prescription information with anyone other than
the patient without specific authorization; this includes family members.

SLIDE 18 | Medication Storage Requirements
Most medications are stored at controlled room temperature (59-77 degrees F). Some
require refrigeration (36-46 degrees F), such as insulin, vaccines, and certain eye
drops. Frozen medications must be kept at -13 to 14 degrees F. Light-sensitive
medications are stored in amber vials or opaque packaging. Nitroglycerin must be
stored in glass containers with a metal cap. Humidity must be controlled to prevent
degradation.
NARRATION: Insulin stored at room temperature longer than 28 days loses potency;
always check storage requirements for each medication.

SLIDE 19 | Pharmacy Math - Percentage and Concentration
Percent weight-in-weight (w/w) means grams of active ingredient per 100 grams of
preparation. Percent weight-in-volume (w/v) means grams per 100 mL. A 1% hydrocortisone
cream contains 1 g of hydrocortisone per 100 g of cream. Alligation is used to
calculate the proportion of two concentrations to mix in order to achieve a desired
intermediate concentration. Specific gravity relates weight to volume for liquids.
NARRATION: Alligation is the compounding technician's most powerful math tool; use it
when you need to combine two different concentrations of the same drug.

SLIDE 20 | Pharmacy Technician Certification Exams
The PTCE (Pharmacy Technician Certification Exam) is administered by PTCB (Pharmacy
Technician Certification Board). It has 90 questions (80 scored, 10 unscored) answered
in 110 minutes, with a passing score of 1400 on a 1600-point scale. The ExCPT (Exam
for the Certification of Pharmacy Technicians) is administered by NHA and covers the
same content domains. Both exams require renewal every two years with continuing
education.
NARRATION: The PTCE and ExCPT cover the same core content; choose based on your
employer's preference or state requirements.

SLIDE 21 | Pharmacology Basics - Drug Actions
Drugs produce their effects by binding to receptors, enzymes, or transport proteins.
Agonists activate receptors to produce a response; antagonists block receptors to
prevent a response. Partial agonists produce a submaximal response. Drug potency
refers to the dose required for a given effect; drug efficacy refers to the maximum
effect achievable. Understanding these concepts helps technicians interpret drug
interaction alerts and recognize why certain drug combinations are prescribed.
NARRATION: Knowing basic pharmacology helps you understand why drugs interact; an
antagonist that blocks a receptor will oppose the effect of an agonist for the same
receptor.

SLIDE 22 | Medication Reconciliation and Transitions of Care
Medication reconciliation compares a patient's medication orders at every transition
of care (admission, transfer, discharge) to identify discrepancies. Hospital pharmacy
technicians often assist pharmacists with reconciliation by gathering medication
histories from patients and care facilities. Errors at care transitions are a leading
cause of adverse drug events. Accurate medication lists prevent duplicate therapy,
omissions, and dangerous interactions after discharge.
NARRATION: Medication reconciliation saves lives at care transitions; accurate
medication history gathering is one of the most important skills a hospital technician
can develop.

SLIDE 23 | Specialty Pharmacy and High-Alert Medications
Specialty pharmacies dispense complex, often injectable medications for conditions
such as cancer, multiple sclerosis, HIV, and rheumatoid arthritis. These drugs are
expensive, require special handling, and often need patient education and monitoring.
High-alert medications (insulin, anticoagulants, concentrated electrolytes, opioids,
and chemotherapy) have the highest risk of causing serious harm when used in error.
ISMP guidelines require double-checks and special storage for high-alert drugs.
NARRATION: Chemotherapy and anticoagulants are the most frequently cited high-alert
drugs in error reports; pharmacies require independent double-checks before dispensing.

SLIDE 24 | Drug Recalls and Safety Alerts
The FDA issues drug recalls when a medication is found to be defective, contaminated,
or mislabeled. Class I recalls involve serious health risks or death; Class II involve
temporary adverse effects; Class III involve no significant health hazard. Pharmacies
must identify and quarantine recalled products immediately, notify affected patients,
and document the response. Technicians play a key role in checking inventory against
recall notices.
NARRATION: When a Class I recall notice arrives, stop dispensing the recalled lot
immediately and contact affected patients right away.

SLIDE 25 | Technician Ethics and Professionalism
Pharmacy technicians must maintain honesty, integrity, and confidentiality. Never
substitute an unapproved generic, falsify records, or override a safety alert without
authorization. Report suspected drug diversion to the pharmacist or compliance officer.
Self-diversion (stealing controlled substances) is a federal crime resulting in loss
of certification, license, and employment, plus criminal prosecution. Uphold
professional boundaries with patients and coworkers at all times.
NARRATION: Drug diversion destroys careers and harms patients; report suspicions
through proper channels rather than looking the other way.

SLIDE 26 | Exam Preparation Strategies
Begin studying at least eight weeks before your exam date. Use PTCB's official
practice exam and the NHA ExCPT practice tests. Focus on dosage calculations,
the top 200 drugs, controlled substance law, and compounding standards, which are
consistently high-weighted content areas. Create flashcards for drug brand/generic
pairs and DEA schedules. Join pharmacy technician study groups online. Review
your weak areas weekly until you consistently score 80% or above on practice exams.
NARRATION: Pharmacy math is the area where most candidates struggle; practice
calculations daily until they feel automatic.
